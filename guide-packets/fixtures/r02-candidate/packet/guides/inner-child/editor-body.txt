The child remains. What can change is who leads. A lot of people stay identified as the child, meeting adult life from the position that still feels small, abandoned, helpless, impulsive, frightened, or desperate to be chosen.</p><p>My answer has been to stop asking the child to heal itself. The present-day person becomes the Nurturer, Protector, and Leader or Guide who reparents the child.</p><p>But that creates a chicken-and-egg problem. What if you’re so identified as the child that you can’t summon those adult archetypes? The method works most easily for people who already have some access to them. Somebody with very little access may sit there trying to sound loving while the inherited critic, numbness, panic, or urge to escape runs the entire session.</p><p>I think the missing first phase is <strong>borrowed adulthood</strong>. Before you can reliably become the inner parent, you may need to experience, imagine, imitate, and practice one adult function with help. You receive care, observe how it is given, participate in it, initiate a small part yourself, and gradually internalize it.</p><p>I developed this practical map from the rest of the method. Treat the complete sequence as a provisional synthesis: some components have direct research behind them, while the full arrangement is my own:</p><ol><li><p><strong>The child alone:</strong> orient to the present and establish enough outside safety to begin.</p></li><li><p><strong>The child with a borrowed adult:</strong> experience one reliable adult function through a real, remembered, imagined, or symbolic figure.</p></li><li><p><strong>The adult apprentice:</strong> care for the child together with support and do at least one part yourself.</p></li><li><p><strong>The inner adult:</strong> repeat warmth, protection, and direction until those capacities become familiar enough to summon.</p></li><li><p><strong>The adult and child in relationship:</strong> the adult leads without exiling the child, and the child no longer has to run the whole life alone.</p></li></ol><p>You don’t have to stop being the child. You become larger than the child-state. A useful sentence is: <em>“I am still this child, and I am also becoming the adult who stays.”</em></p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>My Journey</h1><p>I used to preach <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://mindset.u-dont-exist.com/">Buddhism and enlightenment</a> a lot. Eventually I realized that most people chase spiritual freedom as a way to get above childhood trauma or character defects they don’t want to meet. The usual name is <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://bypassing.u-dont-exist.com/"><em>spiritual bypassing</em></a>. It produces a split personality: the spiritual identity loses awareness of the trauma while the buried material festers and runs the person from underneath. Plenty of seekers and gurus show the split too; I’ve written about the <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://substack.com/@udontexist/note/c-245314772?r=5vdc6m">Dalai Lama as one example</a>.</p><p>Intensive meditation retreats can make this worse, especially <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://open.substack.com/pub/ibogaqueen/p/love-each-other-instead-of-goenka?utm_source=share&amp;utm_medium=android&amp;r=5vdc6m">cultish ones</a>. A person can become exquisitely aware of breath, impermanence, or cosmic love while remaining almost unreachable around the childhood wound directing their relationships.</p><p>Spiritual practice can continue while trauma heals. Divine love can help heal the wounded inner child when you direct it there. When the love stays above the wound, it reinforces the split.</p><p>My own version was strange. I was well-loved as a child, but from age twelve to twenty-six I was depressed and cried every night from feeling the pain of the world. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://love.u-dont-exist.com/">Loving-kindness meditation</a> healed that depression almost instantly. There was already a great deal of love underneath it—from my parents, Buddhist teachings (<a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://substack.com/profile/355021294-u-dont-existcom/note/c-297324975">which it turns out also include inner-child self-love!</a>), and <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://soma.u-dont-exist.com/">loveyhuasca</a>. The meditation gave that love somewhere to land.</p><p>It still didn’t reach all of my inner-child material, especially my attachment to women. The <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://youtu.be/yEWFjODXJbI?si=IXUqrhExojOm6Itm">self-directed inner-child therapy</a> I tried later felt imaginary at first. San Pedro cactus, CBD tea, and sublingual mapacho cracked it open; structured sessions with an inner-child therapist deepened it.</p><p>Spiritual practice without inner-child healing can become bypass. Inner-child healing without spiritual grounding can become endless processing with no direction. I use both. The method now has four elements: a wounded child, three adult functions, protective parts that may stand at the door, and a bootstrap phase for people who can’t yet occupy the adult role.</p><p>The image below maps the three adult functions. So “this feels fake” may mean the person needs a bridge rather than a more forceful performance of love.</p><h2>Borrow Love and Perspective You Already Have</h2><p>Sometimes the missing capacity is not love itself. You can love other people or animals easily, but the feeling becomes inaccessible or unsafe when the recipient is you.</p><p>Think of a pet, a baby, a friend, your own child, a partner, or anyone you naturally love. Feel the actual love first, without immediately trying to turn it into self-love. Let the body remember what the feeling is like. Then see whether a little of it can move toward the younger self.</p><p>Don’t force the transfer. Even recognizing that the capacity exists somewhere in you is useful. You are borrowing a familiar pathway rather than manufacturing a feeling on command.</p><p>Another shortcut is to ask:</p><p><strong>“What would I tell my best friend if they were in this exact situation?”</strong></p><p>It is amazing how much people wisen up when they ask this. They often offer a friend warmth, perspective, protection, patience, and practical advice that they refuse to offer themselves. They take themselves for granted or treat themselves as the one person who does not deserve the standards of care they already understand.</p><p>Notice what you would say to the friend. Which adult function is speaking—the Nurturer, Protector, or Guide? Borrow one sentence or one action from that response and do five percent of it for yourself.</p><p>You do not have to feel fully convinced. The first adult act may simply be refusing to treat yourself worse than you would treat somebody you love.</p><h2>Become the Adult Apprentice</h2><p>The borrowed adult should stand beside you rather than replace you. The movement is: <strong>receive care → observe care → participate in care → initiate care → internalize care</strong>.</p><p>After a therapist, peer, friend, or imagined figure helps, ask:</p><ul><li><p>What did they do that felt warm, protective, or wise?</p></li><li><p>Which five percent of that can I do myself?</p></li><li><p>What action will prove that some of this capacity now belongs to me?</p></li></ul><p>The helper gradually hands the role back. Otherwise borrowed adulthood becomes another authority dependency, and the person remains the child waiting for the good parent to arrive from outside.</p><p>Keep the borrowing narrow. Somebody can model warmth without becoming an authority over your memories, relationships, medicine, spiritual life, or future. Feeling safe with a person in one moment does not make everything they believe wise.</p><p>There is no universal timeline. Look for ordinary signs that the capacity is becoming yours: you can do one protective act without the helper present; disagree with them without losing your ground; recover after missing a practice; ask for help without handing over judgment; and leave a relationship or method that starts demanding obedience. Don’t wait until you feel completely adult. Once you can carry five percent, test that five percent in daily life.</p><p>If the word <em>parent</em> summons the wrong archetype, use safe adult, guardian, advocate, older sibling, wise aunt, good host, captain, or future self. The label matters less than the functions: warmth, protection, and direction.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>The Three Adult Functions</h1><p>I call the deeper therapy <em>pl/ork</em>: play + work. Calling it only “work” forgets the inner child, who also wants to play.</p><p>Most people are stuck identifying as the inner child, which can leave them wandering helplessly through adult life from the place that feels small, frightened, abandoned, impulsive, or helpless. Reparenting begins when the present-day person occupies an adult position and cares for the child from there.</p><p>These are three functions of one adult, not three separate inner people. One act can carry more than one function.</p><ul><li><p><strong>Nurturer:</strong> receives pain with warmth. “It’s okay to feel this. You don’t have to do anything. I’m here.” The feeling is welcomed rather than forced away.</p></li><li><p><strong>Protector:</strong> makes the warmth believable through boundaries, competence, and ordinary action.</p></li><li><p><strong>Leader, Guide, or Guru:</strong> holds standards, chooses direction, and helps the child grow toward a whole life. I consider this the most important and most easily misread function.</p></li></ul><p>The adult position keeps the child inside the relationship. The healed child carries qualities adults often lose: intrinsic and unconditional worth, vivacity, cuteness, silliness, irreverence, rawness, creativity, originality, innocent love and trust, intuition, and an unexplained connection to the Great Mystery before a chosen path gives it a name.</p><p>The child still brings the spark of life. Say no to something costly. Handle an avoided task or financial mess. Leave a relationship that keeps eroding safety. Keep one small promise. Clean your room. Care may first appear as a locked door, a meal, a cancelled obligation, or a phone put down at midnight. Warmth can come later.</p><h2>When Love Is There but Doesn’t Feel Safe</h2><p>Sometimes love is available and still feels unsafe because the younger state distrusts the person offering it. It looks at the grown-up life it can see—old promises, continuing self-attack, repeated failures of protection—and treats that as evidence.</p><p>The child thinks it already has a track record to judge, and it doesn’t like what it sees. Hear the judgment without automatically accepting every conclusion. Separate the ages involved: a small child, an adolescent, a younger adult, and a present child-state had different knowledge, support, freedom, and responsibility.</p><p>Also check who is speaking. The resentful voice and the part trying to make a vow may be the same position, two different positions, or a blend. Don’t merge them just because both are coming through the grown-up you.</p><p><em>“Big fuckity whoopty doo—what are you gonna do for me?”</em> is sarcasm and a literal question. Relaxation may lower the charge enough to remain present, while the credibility dispute still needs its own answer.</p><h2>Make a Simple Vow</h2><p>When promises still carry some credibility, a quiet vow can help. You can make it with or without a visualization of the two of you playing on a swing:</p><blockquote><p>“I know you’re in there. I know I’ve ignored you, and I know you don’t trust me. That’s okay. You don’t have to do anything. My job, starting today, is to build a life that is safe for you. I will start listening. I will start protecting us.”</p></blockquote><p>If the child already sees a negative track record, don’t use the vow to argue with that judgment. You can say, <em>“You’re right that words aren’t enough. You don’t have to trust this. Trust grows from consistency, compassion, and action rather than one beautiful session.</p><h3>Give the Vow a Physical Reminder</h3><p>You can give the promise a physical place in the room: a childhood toy, familiar stuffed animal, visible self-love note, or another object that feels steady and kind.</p><p>Choose what it means while you’re sober and regulated. Let it remind you of the Nurturer or Protector you’re practicing becoming. It doesn’t speak for the child, contain secret wisdom, or become an oracle. Your adult judgment stays in charge.</p><p>Pair the object with one ordinary promise. A stuffed animal might sit beside a note saying, “When I see you, I remember to stop attacking myself and go to bed on time.” Symbolic comfort becomes more believable when the Protector follows through.</p><p>A calm pet can offer companionship while you settle, but the animal is a living companion rather than a prop. Let them come and go without making them responsible for the session.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>When the Adult Voice Feels Fake</h1><p>This is where many people get stuck. You activate the Nurturer, try to speak to the inner child, and get silence. Or numbness. Or a voice saying, “This is so silly.” Suddenly the phone needs checking, dinner needs planning, and a minor household task becomes emotionally urgent.</p><p>My friend Scott said:</p><blockquote><p>“Half the time when I try to connect to baby me, I feel fake and it seems crazy.”</p></blockquote><p>I used to think the answer was mainly persistence: keep showing up and eventually the child will trust you. There’s some truth in that. A child expecting abandonment probably won’t place full confidence in one meditation. Persistence alone still misses what is happening when the whole thing feels fake.</p><h2>The Parent You Inherited</h2><p>Before you deliberately build the Nurturer, Protector, and Guide, you usually already have another inner voice: the parent you inherited from how you were treated. It can be warm, but for many people its first version is a critic or warden: don’t cry, don’t be needy, don’t get angry, don’t embarrass us, don’t become too independent. Piece by piece, the child learns which qualities have to disappear from view to preserve belonging.</p><p>The inherited parent compares the outward persona with an impossible ideal, then attacks from both sides: you aren’t good enough, and whatever looks good is fake anyway. No wonder the inner child doesn’t immediately answer when a new adult voice appears and says, “Trust me.”</p><p>Borrowed adulthood helps because the new voice does not have to win an internal election on the first day. It can arrive as an outside relationship, written plan, or concrete protective act and earn credibility through repetition.</p><h2>Start With Whatever Showed Up</h2><p>The cynical voice, numbness, urge to scroll, anger, dissociation, or sudden need to do something else all belong inside the pl/ork. The response may be a protector, the child speaking directly, an inherited critic, a present-day grievance, or a blend. Start with what it is trying to prevent, accomplish, or communicate before deciding who is speaking.</p><p>Pushing past the response can confirm that you still don’t listen when part of you says no.</p><p>You might say:</p><blockquote><p>“I notice you. I’m not going to force past you. What are you trying to prevent, accomplish, or tell me?”</p></blockquote><p>Then wait. Don’t interrogate the silence. Don’t manufacture an answer because this was supposed to be a productive healing session and you already lit the candle.</p><h3>Two Common Protective Patterns</h3><p>Two patterns often do have a protective function. <strong>Proactive protection</strong> stays on duty most of the time: the skeptic, intellectualizer, inner critic, or voice watching for weakness and self-indulgence. It may have spent years preventing humiliation, deception, overwhelm, or another abandonment. It has probably watched you begin other healing methods with great enthusiasm and then disappear. It may be tired, but it is experienced and competent.</p><p><strong>Reactive protection</strong> often arrives when pain gets close. You suddenly want to drink, smoke, eat, scroll, text somebody, plan something, fix something, sleep, dissociate, get angry, or act out sexually. The response may be trying to step in because nothing else feels able to hold what is coming up.</p><p>Both often grew around an attempt to prevent humiliation, deception, overwhelm, abandonment, or loss of control, even when the current strategy is harmful. Treating the response as an enemy, “block,” or moral failure usually gives it another reason to remain in charge.</p><h3>A Bottom-Up Sequence</h3><ol><li><p><strong>Notice the protective part.</strong> Don’t push through it.</p></li><li><p><strong>Offer it the same Nurturer voice you intended for the child.</strong> “I see you. I know you’re trying to protect something. I want to understand.” Borrow that voice from somebody safe when you can’t generate it yourself.</p></li><li><p><strong>Ask what it expects will happen if it steps back.</strong> The answer may be: “This will become another performance, and you’ll lie to yourself again.” Or: “If you really feel that pain, it will destroy you.” Or: “You tried this before. It didn’t work. We can’t survive another disappointment.”</p></li><li><p><strong>Acknowledge the fear before answering it.</strong> The protective part may be describing your history accurately. The Nurturer receives what it has been carrying rather than winning an argument.</p></li><li><p><strong>Let the Protector build trust through ordinary life.</strong> Keep a promise. Set the boundary. Handle the task. Leave the situation that keeps injuring you. Make your actual life safer.</p></li></ol><p>Real-world competence eventually convinces protective parts that a trustworthy adult has arrived. One compassionate internal speech usually won’t outweigh years of contrary evidence.</p><p>Only after the protective layer feels heard does the inner child often come forward. Push past the guards and you may produce a convincing performance of inner-child pl/ork—the right words, the right visualization, perhaps even the right tears—while nothing underneath feels safer.</p><h3>When the Urge to Escape Arrives</h3><p>Sometimes the protective response is a sudden desire for a substance, a relationship, a new project, or anything else that can move attention away from pain. Don’t shame the urge. Notice what it may be signaling: something painful has come close enough to require management. That can be progress.</p><p>Thank the protective part. Slow down. Bring in support when the material is bigger than your present capacity.</p><p>This is also why I caution against forcing your way into intensive meditation, plant medicine, or aggressive trauma processing before enough Nurturer and Protector capacity exists. Those tools can break through protective layers faster than you can build the ability to hold what comes through.</p><p>Listening to the guards is part of the therapy.</p><p>Gentle meditation or self-hypnosis can still help. Who am I when nobody is grading me?”</p><p>Identity grows through trial and error: choosing clothes, hobbies, friends, ideas, and roles, then changing your mind. When curiosity or mistakes bring ridicule, punishment, or withdrawal of love, the performed self gets better at surviving while the less-practiced self remains blurry.</p><p>Recovery includes more than uncovering a fully formed “true self” hidden underneath. Some of it is developmental pl/ork you get to do late: creating enough safety for preference, curiosity, and experimentation to finally grow.</p><p>Possible signs include:</p><ul><li><p>Self-love practice feels performative; you do the technique and nothing lands.</p></li><li><p>You know what you want mainly through somebody else’s desire, disapproval, or emotional cues.</p></li><li><p>Your preferences, beliefs, and feelings change dramatically with the people around you.</p></li><li><p>You don’t know what you would choose without the fear of disappointing somebody.</p></li><li><p>“What does the child feel?” produces a guessed answer rather than a felt one.</p></li></ul><p>The early practice is finding out who is actually in there: long reflection without external input, private journaling written without imagining a reader, time alone in nature, and the recovery of buried preferences—foods you really like, activities that feel good, beliefs you hold privately, and feelings that appear when nobody else is supplying the emotional cues.</p><p>Focusing can be especially useful because the felt sense may know what the conditioned self was trained to forget. Some people also benefit from temporary, intentional distance long enough for the conditioning to quiet down and authentic preferences to surface.</p><p>Reparenting and identity-building can proceed together. For some people the child-self is already easy to feel. For others, creating the conditions for it to emerge is the first phase.</p><h2>Untangling the Belonging Bargains</h2><p>Identity formation also requires untangling from family emotional systems, religious orthodoxies, group identities, political tribes, and individual relationships that pull you away from your own knowing in exchange for belonging.</p><p>Most social systems ask for some conformity. Families want a particular kind of son, daughter, or sibling. Religions want particular beliefs and behavior. Political tribes expect consistent positions and predictable reactions. Friend groups develop rules about acceptable topics, humor, and emotion. A relationship can require one performance of self to keep the bond intact.</p><p>When value isn’t felt internally, a person can become a “human doer”: worth has to be earned in an external arena. It may be beauty, athleticism, or sexual desirability; intelligence and grades; money, status, or a sophisticated spiritual identity; or becoming indispensable to everybody else’s problems. In its controlling form, caretaking can drift into keeping other people dependent, because being needed has become the caretaker’s main source of value.</p><p>Beauty, intelligence, achievement, and caretaking can all be real gifts. They become belonging bargains when failure in the arena feels like loss of self.</p><p>The unparented child takes those bargains because belonging feels like life or death. Self-differentiation means noticing where belonging is being purchased with self-betrayal and learning to keep your own ground.</p><p>Differentiation can happen inside family, religion, community, or relationship. Selective belonging still leaves room to belong. A differentiated adult can remain deeply embedded while retaining the freedom to question, disagree, or leave when staying would require disappearing. Families with individuated members are healthier than enmeshed families; autonomous faith is healthier than religious compliance; relationships between differentiated people are sturdier than mutual absorption.</p><p>In ordinary life, the movement looks like this:</p><ul><li><p>Other people’s emotions and opinions stop automatically replacing your own.</p></li><li><p>You notice when you agree in the moment and disagree later.</p></li><li><p>You can care about somebody’s feelings without sacrificing your own ground.</p></li><li><p>You can disappoint people, withstand social pressure, and remain recognizably yourself across settings.</p></li><li><p>You can love people in a family, religion, relationship, or political group without becoming the group identity, and question what you were programmed to believe while holding the discomfort.</p></li></ul><p>Differentiation is slow and lifelong. Temporary distance may be necessary when a system won’t allow it from inside. Some people return later on different terms. Sometimes the leaving is permanent because returning would mean disappearing again. The differentiated adult learns to tell the difference.</p><p>Key developed this identity and differentiation portion of the method, drawing on Bowen’s family-systems work and her own self-therapy.</p><h2>Let the Child Be Bad at Things</h2><p>Reflection can reveal what was suppressed, but identity also needs experiments. Try painting, dancing, gardening, a language, a sport, volunteering, music, or something else that has no obvious value to your résumé or social position. Notice what holds your attention when nobody is impressed.</p><p>The old internal parent may demand immediate talent. The first awkward attempt becomes evidence that you are stupid, clumsy, or not “really” that kind of person. The adult has to protect the child from that verdict: “You’re allowed to be bad at this. We are finding out whether we enjoy it, not proving our worth.”</p><p>Give a new interest enough repetitions to get past the humiliation of being a beginner. You may quit because you genuinely dislike it. You may also discover a part of yourself that never had permission to exist. This is pl/ork in its most literal form: play becomes part of identity formation.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>The Inner Guide Comes Later</h1><p>Key adds the third adult function: the Leader, Guide, or Guru. A competent parent does more than soothe and shield. The parent chooses education and experience, holds standards, requires difficult things for growth, and exposes the child to art, music, beauty, nature, and the wider world.</p><p>Without direction, reparenting can become permissive. The child receives attention but doesn’t develop. Candy, screens, and escape from anything difficult can masquerade as self-love.</p><p>The Guide points toward a belonging that doesn’t require self-betrayal: nature, existence itself, or whatever your tradition recognizes as ultimate—God, the divine, the universe, source, the Three Jewels, the Tao, life. Time in nature and contemplative practice make this more than another idea.</p><p>That ground can make social belonging less desperate. You can love people without becoming the group, stay when staying serves life, and leave when staying requires disappearance.</p><p>“Guru” carries spiritual weight for some readers and cult-survivor associations for others. Leader, Guide, Wise One, Sage, Inner Teacher, or Director are all fine. The internal Guide points beyond itself toward what is larger than the social and protects the child from mistaking conditional approval for the deepest belonging. An external guru who demands surrender places themselves where the source should be.</p><p>Most people need the Nurturer and Protector first. Direction requires enough stability for the child to be guided rather than spiritually overruled. Activated too early, the Guru becomes spiritual bypass wearing the clothes of direction. Build warmth and safety first. Let direction emerge once there is a coherent child to orient.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>Simple Practices</h1><h2>A Heart-to-Child Loop</h2><p>If you can feel even a little warmth, put one hand on the center of your chest, at the heart chakra, and feel for love. Put the other on the solar plexus, just below the chest, and feel for the inner child.</p><p>The solar plexus is the most vulnerable part of the torso. One punch there hurts a lot, as I sadly learned while getting beaten up every day at kindergarten recess by a gang organized by my best friend. Can you believe it? So it feels natural to locate the vulnerable inner child in that region.</p><p>Imagine love moving from the heart to the child, then receiving love from the child and sending it back to the heart. Let it become a loop rather than a one-way rescue.</p><p>If warmth is absent, don’t fake it. Place the hands, notice what is there, and borrow one simple sentence from a safe adult: “I won’t attack you while we sit here.” Non-cruelty is enough for a first session.</p><h2>Journal With Each Hand</h2><p>Write vulnerable thoughts and feelings with your non-dominant hand—the clumsy hand whose script already looks like a child wrote it. Answer with the dominant hand as the adult, offering love, protection, and guidance.</p><p>This can bypass the silence people hit with speech or visualization. If the protector is what appears, let that part use the non-dominant hand first. Don’t demand the child before the guard has spoken.</p><h2>Use Childhood Photographs Without Interrogating Them</h2><p>A childhood photograph can help when visualization feels fake. You don’t have to invent the child’s face or posture; you can look at an image from that time. Look gently and notice what arises without deciding what the picture is supposed to prove.</p><p>Useful questions include:</p><ul><li><p>What do I notice in my body?</p></li><li><p>What feeling, impulse, or association arises?</p></li><li><p>What does the younger part seem to want me to understand?</p></li><li><p>What do I remember directly?</p></li><li><p>What was I told later by somebody else?</p></li><li><p>What am I inferring from the photograph?</p></li><li><p>What may be an image, dream, metaphor, altered-state impression, or uncertainty?</p></li><li><p>What can the adult do for the younger part now?</p></li></ul><p>Don’t ask, “What abuse must have happened?” “Who did this to me?” or “What memory is my body hiding?” Don’t keep questioning one photograph until a story finally appears. That caution isn’t theoretical: <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33586291/">guided imagery and suggestive statements can produce false autobiographical beliefs or memories</a>, and <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37160683/">doctored childhood photographs have produced them in experiments</a>. A photograph records one visible moment, chosen and framed by somebody; it doesn’t reveal everything outside the frame or explain why a child looked a certain way.</p><p>Emotional truth and historical fact are different questions. A photograph, bodily reaction, dream, hypnotic image, or entheogenic vision may show something important about your present emotional world without proving that its apparent story literally happened.</p><p>Keep the sources separate: direct memory, something another person told you, inference, image, metaphor, dream, and uncertainty. If a serious allegation begins to form, don’t confront or accuse somebody based only on material produced in this practice. Write down exactly what arose and how it arose, then seek grounded support from somebody who won’t lead you toward a preferred answer.</p><p>Put the photograph away when the practice is done. If it starts pulling you into compulsive searching, certainty, or dysregulation, stop using it for now and return to present-day regulation.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>When Love Still Feels Missing</h1><p>If you weren’t well-loved as a child, “love your inner child” may sound like nonsense. You may sincerely want the best for the child, hug them in imagination, or miss them—and still feel none of the beautiful warmth people mean by love.</p><p>Begin with smaller recognitions:</p><ul><li><p><strong>Love may already be familiar in another form.</strong> Most people have felt some form of it: warmth with a friend, care for a pet, or deep appreciation of beauty. Self-love can begin by turning an existing capacity inward.</p></li><li><p><strong>Love is also practice.</strong> Sleep enough, set a boundary, stop speaking to yourself cruelly, and choose what is good for you. You can act lovingly before the feeling arrives. The Protector can carry part of the Nurturer’s role while the Nurturer comes online.</p></li><li><p><strong>Begin with the absence of cruelty.</strong> Stop the self-attack, shame, and impossible standards. Removing hostility creates room for warmth.</p></li><li><p><strong>Witness love in other people.</strong> Therapy, friendship, community, books, and stories can teach the logic of love until you begin to internalize it.</p></li><li><p><strong>The question matters.</strong> Asking “How do I love myself?” already contains a movement toward something better.</p></li></ul><p>What people most often report helping is surrendering defenses. For somebody who has never felt safe in love, the ego can function like a rigid security guard. Vulnerability is the opening the feeling needs.</p><p>Meeting the protector is one route: ask the cynical or cold part what it is preventing and let it step back by choice. The warmth may be eclipsed rather than absent. Two other approaches come up repeatedly in spiritual and trauma-recovery communities.</p><h2>Hitting the Wall: Radical Acceptance</h2><p>This appears in twelve-step traditions and Zen: stop the self-improvement project for a moment. The effort to fix yourself so you can become worthy keeps reinforcing the belief that you are currently broken.</p><p>Sit with the loneliness, numbness, or emptiness and say: <em>“This is it. If I never feel love for the rest of my life, I will sit here with this void.”</em></p><p>When the fight with the void genuinely stops, the energy used to maintain the fight can release. People describe the void becoming peaceful and sometimes turning into quiet warmth.</p><h2>Somatic Heart Opening</h2><p>Rumi says you must keep breaking your heart until it stays open. The original mechanism language here locates the feeling around the ventral-vagal system and cardiac plexus: some people find that deep grieving eventually drops the nervous system into social engagement and gives way to a physical sense of being held by the universe—a biological rebound.</p><p>Deep primal crying can also overwhelm or retraumatize, especially without support or when a person becomes completely identified with the sadness. Slow down when the body is losing capacity rather than gaining it.</p><h2>The “I Am” Contemplation</h2><p>Advaita Vedanta and non-duality traditions use a different route. Strip away name, job, history, and the identity of “the unloved person.” Attend to the simple fact that you exist. Don’t get stuck at the “I Am” phase; <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://stages.u-dont-exist.com/">it’s preliminary</a>.</p><p>The opening comes when existence stops feeling like a neutral fact and begins to feel like infinite hospitality: the universe is hosting you right now. Gratitude for Being can appear independently of personal history.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>Altered States Can Deepen the Therapy</h1><p>Learn to do inner-child therapy sober, in your ordinary mindstate. When you’re ready, my <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://hypnosis.u-dont-exist.com/"><strong>self-hypnosis guide</strong></a> or <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://lucid.u-dont-exist.com/">lucid-dreaming guide</a> can take it deeper. Low doses of entheogens can also synergize: cactus, CBD tea with sublingual mapacho, <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://loveyhuasca.info/">Loveyhuasca</a>, <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://iboga.u-dont-exist.com/">iboga</a>, and others.</p><p>The gate matters. Altered states can get past the guards before the adult functions are ready to hold what appears. Build sober Nurturer and Protector capacity first, or borrow those capacities from grounded support that does not seize authority over the experience.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>When the Practice Feels Real but Stays Shallow</h1><p>Some people have the opposite problem: they believe they’re connecting when the contact is staying near the surface. Meditating for hours and then sleeping—especially after you have already slept once—can sometimes open much more emotionally direct material.</p><p>What showed me that the daytime pl/ork had stayed shallow was the difference at night. The dream carried far more emotional intensity and unmet need than the conscious session had reached. That contrast was information about the earlier session. It did not make every image or apparent event in the dream a recovered historical fact; dreams and deep states can be symbolic, composite, mistaken, or impossible to verify.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>How to Forgive Without Forgetting</h1><p>For many people, healing eventually includes some form of forgiveness or release from active resentment. It can’t be required on schedule, and it isn’t a prerequisite for safety, accountability, boundaries, justice, or recovery.</p><p>Active resentment can narrow what you see and keep the child organized around the injury. Pressure to forgive early creates an enlightened performance while the wound goes underground.</p><p>The understanding that helps me is seeing how a person’s conditioning, history, pain, and capacity shaped the harmful move they made. Given the total person they had become by that moment, the harmful move was the only one their accumulated nature had available. Hurt people often pass forward pain they never processed. When that becomes something you can actually see rather than a moral instruction, forgiveness may arise by itself, and the rage that held the person as uniquely culpable can soften into something more accurate.</p><p>Accountability remains. The harm is real. Boundaries, legal consequences, moral judgment, distance, and permanent no-contact may all remain appropriate. You can understand the conditioning, hold the person responsible, never speak to them again, and forgive them.</p><p>Self-forgiveness uses the same frame, and difficulty forgiving yourself can also make forgiveness of other people harder. Name what you did without taking responsibility for what wasn’t yours. Let remorse register the inner adult’s recognition that the action violated your values and used the child to do something the child wouldn’t freely choose. Then move through four steps:</p><ol><li><p><strong>Responsibility:</strong> name your action and its impact.</p></li><li><p><strong>Remorse:</strong> let the misalignment with your values register.</p></li><li><p><strong>Rectify:</strong> apologize, make restitution, change the behavior, or prevent repetition where possible.</p></li><li><p><strong>Release:</strong> stop holding yourself hostage after the responsibility has been faced.</p></li></ol><p>The practice is inquiry: How did this person become capable of the harm? What was their child carrying? What moved through them when they hurt you? These questions don’t excuse the action. They make the causal chain visible.</p><p>Some harms take years. Premature forgiveness is one trap; permanent residence inside resentment is another. Don’t push or bypass. Stay with the inquiry until the understanding becomes real enough to change the emotional relationship.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>Borrowed Adulthood in Relationship</h1><p>Reparenting is usually framed as something you do alone or with a therapist. The child was wounded in relationship, though, and a relational injury can only heal so far in isolation.</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/hearthwork-an-integrated-peer-counseling">Hearthwork</a>, my peer-counseling framework, begins: Equal time. Attention without interpretation, advice, or fixing. You speak. I listen. Then we switch. Presence, Warmth, Discipline, Confidentiality. It creates a third kind of relationship alongside ordinary friendship and therapy, one the inner child may need and modern life rarely provides.</p><p>The three adult functions translate outward. A good peer can lend Nurturer through presence and warmth and Protector through confidentiality, time boundaries, and respect for “no.” The peer does not supply your Guide or decide what the experience means. A carefully timed question can help your own Guide become audible without interpretation, advice, or authority over the answer. Then the roles switch.</p><p>This can be borrowed adulthood in its healthiest form. The peer does not become your permanent parent. They demonstrate a capacity, help you participate in it, and return it to you. Afterward, name what you received and do one piece for yourself.</p><p>Therapy remains useful for what therapy does. Community and disciplined peer support do something different. A serious healing path can include both.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>You Are Worthy of Love. Always.</h1><p>Sometimes self-love begins as warmth. Sometimes it begins as borrowed care, a boundary, a meal, a phone put down, or one sentence you are willing to repeat without believing yet.</p><p>You don’t have to feel like a parent before taking the first adult action. The role can begin outside you, become shared, and then become yours. Small, consistent acts of care and protection accumulate into deep healing even when the feeling of love arrives slowly.</p><p>Be patient and gentle. You are more than your pain and your past. You aren’t alone, help exists, and you belong to life, nature, and the whole universe. Love precedes anything you could earn. The child remains alive inside the adult relationship, bringing creativity, tenderness, irreverence, and wonder. They finally have somebody reliable beside them.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>Appendix</h1><h2>How This Relates to IFS</h2><p>Internal Family Systems, or IFS, overlaps with this guide. It recognizes protective parts and teaches people to approach them without shame or combat. The “meet the protector first” sequence is compatible with IFS.</p><p>My reparenting method diverges from IFS at an important point. Standard IFS treats the healer as the Self, a calm and compassionate inner source assumed to be available once parts “unblend.” I emphasize the inner adult you build and practice occupying: Nurturer, Protector, and Leader, Guide, or Guru.</p><p>The borrowed-adulthood phase sharpens the difference. When the calm inner source cannot be reached, the person can borrow one adult function from a relationship, image, plan, or action and gradually internalize it.</p><p>I have no use for another neo-Advaita, <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://goenka.u-dont-exist.com/">spiritual-bypassing</a> claim that a perfect Higher Self is already waiting underneath everything. A trustworthy adult function is built through care, boundaries, action, and direction.</p><p>IFS often calls the wounded part an “exile.” I prefer the inner-child frame: a vulnerable, undeveloped, love-starved, or frozen part that needs parenting and integration rather than a pathological subpersonality.</p><p>IFS “unburdening,” often done after witnessing a part’s origin story, can be powerful. It can also become dramatic, memory-focused, suggestive, or destabilizing—especially for people prone to dissociation, DID-like fragmentation, or spiritual bypass.</p><p>The expanding map of managers, firefighters, exiles, polarized parts, legacy burdens, Self-energy, guides, and new subparts can also become a maze. Some practitioners use the model gently. Others seem to deepen identity fragmentation while trying to resolve it. Institutionalization doesn’t automatically make a framework clearer, safer, or more true.</p><p>A skilled, grounded, non-suggestive, trauma-informed IFS therapist may help, especially when they understand dissociation. For inner-child pain, shame, attachment wounds, self-abandonment, or the absence of a reliable inner adult, a good reparenting therapist may fit better. Understand both the strengths and pitfalls before choosing.</p><h2>Teachers and Practice Resources</h2><p>Several teachers directly influenced this method.
