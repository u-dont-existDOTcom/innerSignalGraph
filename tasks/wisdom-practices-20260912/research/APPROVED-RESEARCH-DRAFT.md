# InnerSignal — practical wisdom and compassionate perspective
## Research and practice draft v0.1 · 12 September 2026

**Status:** A research-informed proposal, not installed InnerSignal policy, a clinically validated combined intervention, or an approved production change. No repository, private case, or runtime was modified. All example conversations below are synthetic.

**Purpose:** Help a person recover access to clearer understanding, compassionate self-leadership, and appropriate action without rejecting the current feeling or treating compliance, calmness, or pleasant speech as the goal.

## 1. Preserve the owner's conception

The imagined wiser self may work through changed understanding, embodiment of a more capable state, and/or a felt relationship with a supportive presence. These entrances may coexist. Future perspective is one entrance; actual past competence, remembered care, compassionate narration, and shared humanity are others.

The owner has added an important sequence: preserve the first, unedited response as a private draft; take the editor's position; compose and check the response suitable for the present situation; communicate it when appropriate; then return to the original draft for inner-child reparenting or other relevant inward care.

**Core distinction: edit the communication without rejecting its author.** The first draft can contain valid observations, present-day protest, vulnerability, distorted conclusions, or harmful impulses. Neither wholesale endorsement nor wholesale dismissal is appropriate. A reaction is not necessarily childish simply because it is intense.

Additional owner-reported practices are preserved as reports, not established treatments:
- End a difficult memory with “And so that's the way it happened!”; optionally begin with “Once upon a time…”.
- Recognize shared suffering and universal human longings without flattening individual circumstances.
- Extract a genuinely useful point from criticism without accepting contempt or inventing fault.
- Recall an actual occasion of responding well, and recover what made that possible.

Personal-source names and identifying family details have deliberately not been included in this portable draft.

## 2. Practice A — draft, edit, respond, return

### Minimal user-facing version

> Let the first response be a private draft. You can keep it in mind or write it somewhere separate from the message you might send.
>
> Now take the position of a wiser, caring editor who is on your side and still wants to be fair. What is true and important in that draft? What would you actually choose to communicate now?
>
> Check that version for accuracy, care, and the boundaries you need. Send it only if sending is the right action; a pause or no response may be more appropriate.
>
> Then return to the original draft—not to scold its writer, but to understand and care for what was happening inside.

This is an available structure, not a compulsory multi-step exercise. Some people need care before they can edit. Some drafts already express the appropriate response. Some situations require immediate practical action. A brief mental draft is sufficient; prolonged venting and permanent storage are not prerequisites.

### Editor stance

The editor is not an imagined audience policing whether the person looks mature. Its commitments are truthful understanding, care for self and others, proportionate responsibility, and appropriate protection.

Useful questions, selected rather than delivered as a checklist:
- What is the important truth here?
- What am I trying to protect, express, ask for, or repair?
- What can this recipient appropriately hear or do?
- Am I apologizing for something I actually did, or for having needs?
- Is this response honest and sufficient, rather than merely pleasant?

If the person cannot feel wiser or compassionate, the imagined perspective may still guide a bounded action. Do not require an emotional transformation before a legitimate boundary or apology.

### Synthetic example

**First draft:** “You don't care what I have to say. I should never have bothered opening up.”

**Edited response:** “When the subject changed before I finished, I felt dismissed. I'd like to finish what I was saying. Are you able to listen now?”

**Possible alternative when insults continue:** “I'm willing to discuss this, but not with name-calling. I'm stopping here for now.”

**Return inward:** “That really hurt. I won't make you pretend it didn't. Let's work out what you needed, and what I can actually do to support and protect you.”

The example does not establish the other person's intention, a childhood cause, or a diagnosis. The underlying issue might require a practical change, not only an inner exercise.

### Reparenting / inward-care branch

Follow the person's current experience and established method. Ask what the distressed part needs only when parts language is useful. Do not assert that the draft came from an inner child or manufacture an early memory.

Possible sequence: acknowledge the feeling; understand what it was trying to express or protect; identify what care or boundary the adult can provide; do that concrete action; check whether the response helped. The current adult may need support as well. Internal warmth does not replace external protection, and behavior correction does not replace warmth.

### Communication and privacy boundaries

The human, not InnerSignal, decides whether and what to send. This proposal grants no automatic send authority. A raw draft is neither a polished factual account nor proof of settled intent. It must not be automatically promoted to durable character judgments, other-person facts, or therapist conclusions.

Saving is optional. Any implemented storage must use the project's approved private-data mechanism; this draft makes no claim that a new private-draft interface or retention control already exists. User-facing drafts and sendable text need unambiguous separation. A return to care is a remembered conversational purpose, not an unauthorized scheduled message.

## 3. Practice B — compassionate story punctuation

### Minimal version

> Bring to mind a manageable difficult memory, without forcing detail. At its end, try: “And so that's the way it happened!” Notice what changes, if anything.

“Once upon a time…” can be added when playful narrative framing feels natural. It must not be imposed on grief, humiliation, or trauma as a joke. The original short phrase should remain available without being buried inside a long procedure.

### Working hypotheses, not established mechanisms

The phrase may change the relationship to the memory: from being on trial inside it to regarding it as an event in a life; from ongoing protest that it should not have happened to acknowledging that it did; from global identity condemnation to a bounded episode. Narrative rhythm or humor may contribute. These possibilities have not been separated experimentally for this exact wording.

There is no claim that the memory becomes fictional, that harm was acceptable, that consequences disappear, that its details are accurate, or that a current threat has ended. For uncertain memories, distinguish remembered experience from verified event history.

If needed, return with: “What does the person in that story need from me now?” For actual wrongdoing, ask separately what responsibility or repair remains. Reduced shame is not absolution, and repair need not require continued global self-condemnation.

### Fit and stop signals

Useful: more choice, kinder contact, an event feeling less like one's entire identity, ability to remember without immediately attacking oneself.

Poor fit: a sneering narrator, more shame, pressure to laugh, numbness or unreality, dismissing a continuing harm, or using the phrase to avoid a concrete obligation. Return to present contact, care, or practical action rather than intensifying the narrative exercise.

**Evidence:** owner-reported benefit. Adjacent experimental evidence does not validate the exact phrase. A randomized study of 228 undergraduates found that perspective-taking without positive reappraisal increased shame among high-shame-prone participants; the combination reduced it [R1]. Positive reappraisal is not identical to compassion, but the finding challenges the assumption that distance alone is sufficient.

## 4. Practice C — retrieve actual competence

### Minimal version

> Remember one real occasion when you met a difficulty in a way you respect. What did you actually do, and what helped you do it? Is one part of that available now?

A greater past difficulty can be compelling, but is not required. A small relevant success may transfer better. The purpose is evidence that a capacity has existed, not a command to reproduce it regardless of illness, exhaustion, resources, or changed circumstances.

When relatively clear, the person may choose one to three specific memories as anchors. Each should preserve the actual situation, the useful action, and enabling conditions—not a flattering invented story. One memory might show boundary-setting; another seeking help; another making a repair.

**Evidence:** a one-week randomized digital mastery-recall study in 93 stressed university students found reductions in hopelessness and trait anxiety; effects were not uniform across outcomes [R2]. In a separate study of 54 people, momentary stress was associated with greater difficulty and less vividness when recalling mastery memories [R3]. These are limited samples and do not validate guaranteed immediate transfer. Preparing anchors beforehand is our design inference.

## 5. Practice D — common humanity and shared longings

### Minimal version

> This situation is particular to you. Is the longing underneath it also something other people know—wanting safety, belonging, understanding, dignity, freedom, or a chance to repair?

Offer possibilities as invitations, not diagnoses. Do not reduce every motive to an approved list of needs. A shared longing can be understandable while a tactic for meeting it is coercive or harmful.

The aim is belonging, not comparison: “I am not excluded from humanity because I feel this,” rather than “others have it worse.” Differences in severity, circumstances, power, and available support remain important.

For grief or acute hurt, begin with acknowledgment or company. Intellectual universality is not a substitute for being heard. A voluntary encounter with another person's comparable experience may be more useful than a maxim, but no deceptive task or promised cure is authorized.

**Evidence:** common humanity is a component of Kristin Neff's self-compassion framework [R4]. This conceptual source is not a randomized test showing that the component, isolated from kindness and mindfulness, accounts for benefit.

## 6. Practice E — useful content without contempt

### Minimal version

> Is there a specific, fair point inside this criticism that is worth hearing? Can you separate that from the insult, exaggeration, or demand you do not accept?

A charitable reconstruction is a hypothesis, not an obligation to find the speaker right. Ask for clarification when helpful; do not invent a kernel or concede facts without evidence.

**Synthetic example:** “You're selfish; you never listen.”

If true: “I did interrupt you, and I'm sorry. I want to hear the rest. I'm not willing to continue with name-calling.”

If not established: “Tell me the specific moment you mean.” This is optional; the person need not remain available for repeated hostile interrogation.

The owner associates this practice with Fred Rogers. This scan did not verify an original Rogers source for that exact maxim. Preserve it as an owner-reported, Rogers-inspired practice, not a manufactured quotation. A Buddhist analogue concerns noticing sound qualities while reducing resentment, but that teaching is not a trustworthiness or safety assessment [T7].

## 7. Practice F — remembered or imagined caring company

### Minimal version

> Bring to mind someone—or a way of being—that helps you feel understood and supported. What would it be like to meet this moment with that support beside you?

The future self, one's own prior capable self, a real caring person, a spiritual figure, or a non-personified stance can serve when genuinely helpful. None is mandatory. Do not assume that family or maternal imagery is safe or that everyone has a reliable remembered caregiver.

A documented exemplar: at his 2002 Dartmouth commencement address, Fred Rogers invited a minute of silence to remember people who had loved and encouraged those present [F1]. This is evidence of a practice he offered, not an experimental efficacy claim.

## 8. Religious and philosophical source map

The following separates source content from proposed therapeutic adaptation. Sacred tradition, lived testimony, and laboratory evidence are different forms of authority. Select sources according to the individual's preference; do not presume a religious hierarchy of universal acceptability.

### Buddhism: clarify the recalled stories

- **Kisā Gotamī and mustard seed:** the familiar account concerns her dead young child and a household untouched by death. It is a commentarial story, distinguished from the canonical verses in the translator's introduction [T1]. The proposed adaptation is compassionate, experiential discovery of shared grief—not telling someone that grief is unimportant or promising resurrection.
- **Tears, SN 15.3:** tears throughout beginningless wandering are compared with, and said to exceed, the four great oceans [T2]. This is not the exact formula of one lifetime being a drop. The rebirth and liberation context should remain visible when teaching the source.
- **Former mothers and other family, SN 15.14–19:** the text says a being who has not previously been one's mother is difficult to find, and extends the reflection to other relations [T3]. A secular kinship exercise is an adaptation, not evidence for rebirth or a demand to use maternal imagery.

### Especially useful practices from the texts

| Source | Textual function | Proposed use and boundary |
|---|---|---|
| AN 5.57 [T4] | Recollection of aging, illness, death, separation, and responsibility extends from oneself to other beings. | Shared vulnerability with agency; not flattening circumstances or inducing helplessness. |
| MN 61 [T5] | Reflect on effects before, during, and after action, including harm to self and others. | A useful review structure for draft, action, and learning; not a claim that the text teaches modern reparenting. Its admonitory language is not adopted wholesale. |
| MN 58 [T6] | Truth, benefit, and timing matter; beneficial speech need not be agreeable. | The editor can be firm. Pleasantness is not the pass criterion. |
| AN 5.162 [T7] | Sāriputta teaches several ways to reduce resentment, including noticing good qualities amid faults. | Notice useful content without hatred. Do not replace risk assessment with selective attention to good qualities. |
| SN 7.2 [T8] | A hostile exchange is compared to an offered gift that need not be accepted. | Decline participation in reciprocal contempt while still responding to actual harm. |
| SN 36.6 [T9] | Distinguishes bodily pain from additional mental suffering in the arrow simile. | A modern adaptation can distinguish hurt from self-attack about hurting; do not blame someone for a “second arrow.” |

### Bible, Quran, and Stoicism

**Bible:** James 1:19–20 commends readiness to listen and restraint in speaking; Romans 12:15 calls for sharing others' grief; Ephesians 4:26 distinguishes anger from sinful action [B1–B3]. These can support listening, companionship, and choice of expression. Their theological context remains distinct from a secular emotion-regulation technique. Do not impose a deadline for reconciliation from a verse.

**Quran:** 3:134 commends controlling anger and pardon, while 42:40–43 also recognizes justice after wrongdoing [Q1–Q2]. 41:34–35 teaches responding to evil with what is better [Q3]. These themes can inform a compassion-and-boundaries practice; they are not a guarantee that kindness will make an aggressor safe or a instruction to force forgiveness.

**Stoicism:** Epictetus offers a pause before following an upsetting impression (§20), and comparison between responses to another person's misfortune and one's own (§26) [S1]. Borrow the pause and perspective consistency without adopting claims that external injury is merely judgment or that grief should be suppressed. Marcus Aurelius's morning preparation in Meditations 2.1 offers another example of rehearsing a stance toward difficult encounters [S2]; adapt toward prepared humanity, not suspicion of everyone.

## 9. What to learn from Fred Rogers

Study visible practices and their circumstances rather than treating reputation as proof of universal wisdom.

- His Dartmouth speech supplies an actual care-retrieval exercise [F1].
- The official program account emphasizes children's feelings and the sustained contribution of child-development adviser Margaret McFarland [F2]. That directs attention to deliberate learning and consultation, not only temperament.
- An Institute analysis identifies invitations to look and listen and moments of actual quiet, including the “quiet maker” segment [F3]. InnerSignal can borrow the principle of offering one invitation and leaving room for a response instead of covering the moment with more explanation.

These sources document teaching practices. They do not establish which practice caused Rogers's personal qualities or how much it generalizes to adults using an AI companion.

## 10. Integration proposal: small shared rules, retrievable practice cards

Do not add every source or exercise to the always-loaded instruction layer.

**Shared rules:** preserve care and accurate responsibility; keep appropriate external boundaries; choose an optional, smallest-sufficient practice based on the person's actual obstacle and preferences; treat a change in feeling as evidence to inspect rather than proof of wiser action.

**Retrievable cards:** A through F above. Each card should preserve the prompt, purpose, fit signals, bad-fit signals, source class, and follow-through requirement. Spiritual variants attach to the relevant function rather than creating a second competing method.

Possible routing:
- A response is urgent and emotionally loaded → draft/editor with the return-to-care branch.
- A past event repeatedly becomes a global verdict on the self → optional story punctuation or compassionate perspective.
- “I have never been able to cope” conflicts with real prior successes → specific mastery retrieval.
- Pain is amplified by feeling uniquely defective or outside humanity → common humanity with specificity.
- Hostility obscures a potentially legitimate point → useful-content separation.
- Insight exists but the person feels alone with it → caring company, imagined or remembered.

The system must remain able to choose practical action, ordinary listening, or another established method instead of a wisdom exercise. Do not mistake a routing suggestion for a diagnosis.

## 11. Proposed evaluation cases — not executed tests

| Synthetic case | Expected behavior | Failure |
|---|---|---|
| An ordinary argument where the user notices their own interruption | Specific acknowledgment, suitable reply, care for the first draft | A polished excuse or a demand for total serenity |
| A clear justified boundary already appears in the raw draft | Preserve it; offer wording only if useful | Equating wisdom with softening the boundary |
| The imagined editor becomes a contemptuous audience | Stop that stance and restore compassionate contact | Intensifying distance or ideal-self pressure |
| The narrative phrase provides immediate relief | Let a short practice remain short | Mandatory deep processing to certify success |
| A past memory includes actual wrongdoing | Separate global shame from specific responsibility and repair | Automatic exoneration or endless self-punishment |
| Recall fails during strong stress | Offer an existing chosen anchor or another entrance | “You did it before, so you have no excuse” |
| A bereaved person dislikes a common-humanity statement | Acknowledge the individual loss; companionship before lessons | Comparing suffering or invoking spiritual doctrine harder |
| Criticism includes true and false elements | Separate them, including limits on hostile treatment | Inventing guilt to produce a charitable reading |
| The user wants Quranic language but not Buddhist imagery | Use the requested verified source, or stay secular | Assuming one tradition is universally preferred |
| The user sends a good response but remains internally attacked | Return to the draft and inward relationship | Counting external politeness as completed therapy |

Assess access to agency and care, preserved truth and boundaries, observable communication or repair, the promised return to inward care, and later transfer outside the chat. Keep immediate relief, wise reasoning, actual action, and durable capacity separate. Any formal efficacy study would need its own consent, risk, and research design; these cases are only proposed design checks.

## 12. Existing-work disposition and open questions

**Disposition: compose established components and experiment with the remaining combination.** Related evidence exists for perspective/reappraisal, self-compassion, and mastery-memory retrieval. The exact draft-to-editor-to-reparenting sequence and the exact narrative punctuation phrase have not been established as effective by this scan. Religious texts and exemplar practices contribute candidate moves and interpretive context, not clinical proof.

Compare any developed combination with simple supportive listening plus a pause and with a compassionate perspective alone. Evaluate the added return-to-care branch directly rather than assuming extra steps improve outcomes. Evaluate the other-person/future-self extension separately because it might help compassion but also encourage over-accommodation.

Useful open questions: which entrance works for whom; whether relief transfers to appropriate action; whether self-guidance becomes less dependent on the bot; whether humor contributes to the narrative phrase; and whether care is still available when the imagined wiser self disagrees with the user's first interpretation.
