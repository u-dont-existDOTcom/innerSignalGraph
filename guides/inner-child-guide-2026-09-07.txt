The Chicken-and-Egg Problem
Céline’s title gets the problem right: we don’t change. The child remains. What can change is who leads. A lot of people stay identified as the child, meeting adult life from the position that still feels small, abandoned, helpless, impulsive, frightened, or desperate to be chosen.

My answer has been to stop asking the child to heal itself. The present-day person becomes the Nurturer, Protector, and Leader or Guide who reparents the child.

But that creates a chicken-and-egg problem. What if you’re so identified as the child that you can’t summon those adult archetypes? The method works most easily for people who already have some access to them. Somebody with very little access may sit there trying to sound loving while the inherited critic, numbness, panic, or urge to escape runs the entire session.

I think the missing first phase is borrowed adulthood. Before you can reliably become the inner parent, you may need to experience, imagine, imitate, and practice one adult function with help. You receive care, observe how it is given, participate in it, initiate a small part yourself, and gradually internalize it.

I developed this practical map from the rest of the method. Treat the complete sequence as a provisional synthesis: some components have direct research behind them, while the full arrangement is my own:

The child alone: orient to the present and establish enough outside safety to begin.

The child with a borrowed adult: experience one reliable adult function through a real, remembered, imagined, or symbolic figure.

The adult apprentice: care for the child together with support and do at least one part yourself.

The inner adult: repeat warmth, protection, and direction until those capacities become familiar enough to summon.

The adult and child in relationship: the adult leads without exiling the child, and the child no longer has to run the whole life alone.

You don’t have to stop being the child. You become larger than the child-state. A useful sentence is: “I am still this child, and I am also becoming the adult who stays.”

My Journey
I used to preach Buddhist enlightenment a lot. Eventually I realized that most people chase spiritual freedom as a way to get above childhood trauma or character defects they don’t want to meet. That’s called spiritual bypassing. It produces a split personality: the spiritual identity loses awareness of the trauma while the buried material festers and runs the person from underneath. Plenty of seekers and gurus show the split too; I’ve written about the Dalai Lama as one example.

The analogy I like to use for understanding this concept is: imagine you’re broke and starving. Your baby also has nothing to eat. Understandable. Now imagine that they opened a nice food bank in your neighborhood and suddenly you’ve got lots of nutritious munchies available. But you’re just devouring them all yourself while baby you is still starving. Unforgivable.

That sounds ridiculous, and nobody would really do that. But because we don’t believe there is a baby us, we do do that. That damages the baby way more than if both of you are starving. Once you realize baby you is in there, you’ll naturally offer him/her the love and freedom you’ve hopefully found on your spiritual path.

People are spiritually bypassing not because they’re stupid or crazy, but because that’s what they figured out so far. I was doing that as well until a few years ago, because I didn’t know about the inner child. It sounded pretty silly to me at first, but through direct experience I discovered he’s really in there! And when you can really reconnect and reparent your inner child, it’s much more of a true embodied healing than an escape hatch.

On the other hand, therapy risks becoming endless processing if the whole horizon is still “how do I manage this wound better?” For me, spirituality opened a much larger horizon of what happiness and love could even mean, beyond conditions & life story.

I don’t think everybody needs the same depth of spiritual experience. Some people already have enough love, meaning, beauty, and belonging for the life they want and aren’t especially curious for more. But for somebody who feels that ordinary life and conditional love are radically insufficient, the question of whether something much deeper is possible can become central.

Don’t Give the Inner Adult Away
In my experience, most people do not start by becoming their own loving parent. They go looking for one. Sometimes it is a spiritual guru; sometimes a therapist, coach, partner, community, or ideology. That can be a healthy first stage. I call it borrowed adulthood. The helper lends a function you cannot yet hold alone. The trouble begins when the helper keeps it.

A spiritual practice can change your relationship to pain. It cannot make an abusive person safe, repair a house, treat an infection, pay a debt, or leave a coercive relationship. Sometimes the adult move is to sit and feel. Sometimes it is to lock the door, call somebody, go to a doctor, get legal advice, or leave. A teaching that turns actual danger into merely ‘your story’ has stopped protecting the child.

No-self teachings can also arrive out of order. If you were never allowed to discover what you like, believe, want, or refuse, you may need to build a self before you start dissolving it. Enough selfhood to stand; then enough flexibility not to become imprisoned by it. Using emptiness to erase an undeveloped self is spiritual bypass wearing very advanced clothes.

The same problem appears with therapist-gurus. A helper may see a blind spot, challenge you, or model a missing adult function. They do not get final authority over your memories, relationships, body, medicine, conscience, or future. Even Pema Chödrön, writing from a tradition that emphasizes devotion, says the teacher should “wean us from dependency altogether” and help us grow up. That is the test I keep. Are you becoming more able to think, disagree, protect yourself, and act without the helper?

Disagreement with a helper is not automatically a defense against healing. Their interpretation may be wrong, their method may not fit, or you may not share their goal. They should be able to examine that without making your objection prove their theory.

Borrow adulthood. Do not surrender it. The right helper stands beside you, teaches what they can, and gradually hands your life back.

My own version was strange. I was well-loved as a child, but from age twelve to twenty-six I was depressed and cried every night from feeling the pain of the world. Loving-kindness (metta) meditation healed that depression almost instantly. There was already a great deal of love underneath it—from my parents, Buddhist teachings (which it turns out also include inner-child self-love!), and loveyhuasca. The meditation gave that love somewhere to land.

It still didn’t reach all of my inner-child material, especially my attachment to women. The self-directed inner-child therapy I tried later felt imaginary at first. San Pedro cactus, CBD tea, and sublingual mapacho cracked it open; structured sessions with an inner-child therapist deepened it.

The method now has four elements: a wounded child, three adult functions, protective parts that may stand at the door, and a bootstrap phase for people who can’t yet occupy the adult role.

Catch the Hook Before You Interpret It
Sometimes the first useful move is not to identify the child, the protector, or the origin of the reaction. Catch the moment the system gets hooked.

The jaw tightens. The belly contracts. Heat rises. Something pulls back. Suddenly you want to attack, explain, text, scroll, drink, disappear, make a dramatic promise, or decide what the whole relationship means.

Pema Chödrön uses the Tibetan word shenpa for this hook: the tightening and urge that arrive before the complete storyline. Try this:

Look around the room and notice: “I am hooked.”

Unless immediate safety requires action, do not speak or act for one to three breaths.

Feel the bodily charge without deciding what it proves.

Ask which adult function is needed now: warmth, protection, or direction.

Take one small adult action, or relax and continue with the day.

The story arising in that moment may be true, partly true, mistaken, or mixed with older material. The hook is not a truth detector. Pausing does not invalidate the story; it keeps an activated state from deciding the entire case.

Acute work asks: What will prevent harm in the next ten minutes? What should I not say, send, consume, promise, or decide yet?

Meaning work can come later: Which younger state or protector was involved? What present grievance is valid? What history does this resemble? What boundary, grief, repair, or developmental need remains?

This is not an endurance test. One breath may be enough. If turning inward makes you less oriented, less functional, or more compelled, stop and use external support.


Also Look Outward
Not every hook is mainly old material. Before analyzing the child or protector, also notice what the other person is actually doing. Can they tolerate disagreement, reflect on their behavior, take responsibility, and make room for your experience—or does the interaction repeatedly end with you carrying their feelings and abandoning your own position?

Lindsay Gibson’s work on emotional immaturity is useful here because it shifts attention from chasing motives to demonstrated relational capacity. Sometimes the problem is not that you have failed to explain yourself well enough. Adjust your expectations to what the person repeatedly shows they can actually offer, rather than what one perfect explanation might finally unlock.

Keep that assessment specific and revisable. Someone disagreeing with you, needing time, or setting their own boundary does not make them emotionally immature. You may not know whether they cannot respond differently, will not, or cannot right now. You can still decide what you cannot currently rely on. Update that judgment when their behavior changes—not merely when they promise. And you do not need repeated exposure to danger before protecting yourself.

Notice your side too. Feeling guilty does not prove that you did something wrong; it also does not prove that somebody manipulated you. Did you cause harm that needs repair, or are you abandoning your position to make their disappointment go away? Sometimes both are present.

Before the next interaction, choose a realistic outcome. Mutual understanding may be possible. Sometimes success is conveying information, saying no, or ending an exchange without forty minutes of self-defense. You do not have to make somebody understand you before you can act.

The Witness notices what is happening inside me. The Protector notices what is happening between us. The Guide asks: “Given both, what is the next sane move?”

When More Processing Becomes the Hook

A real wound and an unhelpful response to it can coexist. You may still have grief to feel while repeatedly checking whether you have grieved correctly. Attend to immediate safety and practical action when needed, but another round of analysis is not automatically more care.

Ask: “Is this helping me feel or understand something more clearly, make a decision, or respond differently—or am I checking the same question for reassurance?” Meaningful grief and practice can revisit familiar ground; novelty is not required every time. But repetition alone does not make a thought a new request from the child.

When it is a checking loop, let the thought be there without arguing with it, chasing it away, or waiting to feel completely resolved. Return to a chosen activity or relationship. You can care for the child without holding a meeting every time the mind produces a sentence.

The exercises here should not become a ritual you must complete before you are allowed to live.

Borrow the Adult Before You Can Be the Adult
Some people can close their eyes, imagine a loving parent, and immediately feel the archetype. Others summon the voice of the person who shamed, controlled, ignored, or frightened them. Some feel nothing. The instruction “be your own loving parent” already assumes the capacity they are missing.

A Witness Is Enough to Begin
The first adult position may be only a witness: “A frightened younger state is here, and something can notice it.” You don’t need warmth, wisdom, or a perfect answer yet. The fact that one part can observe another creates a little space around total identification.

Using your own name can help. Instead of “I am terrified,” try, “A younger part of Joel is terrified. What does Joel need from an adult right now?” Laboratory studies of third-person self-talk suggest that this small change in language can create psychological distance and reduce emotional reactivity without demanding a great deal of conscious control.

The witness is not yet claiming to be a wonderful parent. It is simply refusing to disappear.

If you can already notice and distinguish several internal positions—the younger state, the resentful voice, the part trying to make a promise—you may already have enough witness capacity. Don’t keep repeating observer exercises merely because warmth or protection is still difficult. Move to the adult function that is actually missing.

Borrow One Function at a Time
A complete ideal parent may be impossible to imagine. Borrow one function at a time from a figure who embodies it: a therapist, grandmother, teacher, friend, older sibling, spiritual figure, fictional character, future self, or another presence whose care does not require you to disappear.

Borrowed Nurturer: “How would this person sit beside the child?” Begin with non-cruelty if love feels inaccessible: no insults, no rushing, no impossible standards, no demand that the child perform gratitude for the session.

Borrowed Protector: “What would a minimally competent adult do in the next ten minutes?” Eat, lock the door, stop replying, leave the unsafe room, ask for help, or go to sleep.

Borrowed Guide: “What did I decide when I was calm? What would the wise person I respect ask me to practice today?” Direction can come from a written plan, value, teacher, or tradition until you can hold it yourself.

Borrowing can also help the part trying to be the adult. When the child answers with sarcasm, distrust, or contempt, ask how a decent adult would hear it without defending their goodness, retaliating, collapsing, or leaving. That may be the only function you borrow for the moment.

You may still feel completely like the child while doing the action. That is fine. Adult identity can form after the behavior: “I did that for us. That was the Protector.”

Related research gives this idea some support without proving the whole method. A small pilot of the Ideal Parent Figure method used imagined ideal caregivers during the stabilization phase for adults with childhood-trauma-related complex PTSD. In one experiment, compassion imagery evoked threat responses in highly self-critical participants; another found that a secure-attachment prime reduced some of that response. So “this feels fake” may mean the person needs a bridge rather than a more forceful performance of love.

Borrow Love—or Borrow the Perspective of Care
Sometimes the missing capacity is not love itself. You can love other people or animals easily, but the feeling becomes inaccessible or unsafe when the recipient is you.

Think of a pet, a baby, a friend, your own child, a partner, or anyone you naturally love. Feel the actual love first, without immediately trying to turn it into self-love. Let the body remember what the feeling is like. Then see whether a little of it can move toward the younger self.

Don’t force the transfer. Even recognizing that the capacity exists somewhere in you is useful. You are borrowing a familiar pathway rather than manufacturing a feeling on command.

Notice not just the words, but how they are being said inside. “I care about you” can carry patience or a demand to hurry up and feel better. If imagined closeness feels intrusive, try more distance, no touch, a different voice, or another source of care. Difficulty with this particular offer does not establish an inability to receive love. Let the response teach you how to care, rather than forcing the child to accept your preferred version.

Don’t make this exercise depend on a lucky biography. Some people can’t think of a best friend, loving parent, partner, child, or pet who feels usable as a proxy. In that case, imagine somebody whose intention is simply to take good care of you: an ideal parent, wise aunt, decent therapist, fictional character, future self, or an adult you invent for the purpose. If even that feels fake or threatening, begin with the least cruel, most sane response a minimally decent adult could give. The point is to borrow a caring function, not to prove that somebody like this existed in your past.

Another shortcut is to ask:

“If somebody I cared about had my actual history, limitations, and bad options, what would I say to them?”

If nobody usable comes to mind, ask:

“What would somebody who genuinely wanted to take good care of me say or do next?”

Let this person have your actual history and constraints, not a tidier life where every sensible option was available. What would you say? What would you protect them from? What would you ask them to stop doing today? What small action would you advise next?

It is amazing how much people wisen up when they ask this. Psychologists sometimes call the wider pattern self-distancing or Solomon’s paradox: people often reason more wisely about another person’s conflict than their own, and taking a more distanced perspective can narrow that gap. The exact prompt is my practical version of that idea.

Do not borrow advice merely because it sounds generous. Advice to other people can become too idealistic because the adviser does not have to pay the cost. Keep the part that is compassionate, sane, realistic, and based on the person’s actual options.

Which adult function is speaking—the Nurturer, Protector, or Guide? Borrow one sentence or one action and do five percent of it for yourself. You do not have to feel fully convinced.

Then imagine that the person shrugs, rejects the advice, or says, “Big whoop.” Which part remains true without requiring their gratitude, trust, or agreement? That is often the part your inner adult can borrow without turning care into a demand.

Once you find one usable sentence or action, stop repeating the thought experiment in different words. Try the sentence or do the action.

Ask God for a Loan
Ordinary love and profound spiritual love are both real forms of love, but I don’t want to flatten them into the same experience. Caring about a friend, feeding somebody who is hungry, protecting a child, or wanting an animal to be comfortable may give you exactly the pathway you need to begin. That is real love.

But some people have also experienced a kind of love that feels radically different in depth or scale: divine love, universal love, a heart opening, the love encountered in prayer or meditation, or something revealed in a spiritual or mystical experience. If you already know that territory, don’t reduce it to a thought experiment about how you would treat a friend.

Find whatever access to that love is genuinely available now. Then bring the younger self into the same field.

Don’t force it. If the child goes numb, distrusts the love, feels threatened by it, or says, “Bullshit,” that reaction becomes the pl/ork. Listen to it. The answer is not to pour more spirituality on top.

This is the feast problem. You can have an enormous spiritual feast upstairs while the child is still starving downstairs. Spiritual integration means some of what you have actually reaches them.

When the Spiritual Relationship Hurts

Sometimes the difficulty is not simply accessing love. Prayer may evoke abandonment or condemnation; a teacher’s betrayal may damage trust in what once felt sacred. Don’t automatically call this bypass, lack of faith, or disguised parental conflict. Ask what has been lost, what needs questioning, and what is still worth preserving. You may need to grieve or change a relationship, practice, or understanding. This need not require either abandoning spirituality or forcing more of the same.

Nor does developing an inner adult mean controlling everything yourself. Spiritual support can help you act where responsibility is yours and relinquish outcomes or other people’s choices where control is not yours.


Become the Adult Apprentice
The borrowed adult should stand beside you rather than replace you. The movement is: receive care → observe care → participate in care → initiate care → internalize care.

After a therapist, peer, friend, or imagined figure helps, ask:

What did they do that felt warm, protective, or wise?

Which five percent of that can I do myself?

What action will prove that some of this capacity now belongs to me?

The helper gradually hands the role back. Otherwise borrowed adulthood becomes another authority dependency, and the person remains the child waiting for the good parent to arrive from outside.

Keep the borrowing narrow. Somebody can model warmth without becoming an authority over your memories, relationships, medicine, spiritual life, or future. Feeling safe with a person in one moment does not make everything they believe wise.

There is no universal timeline. Look for ordinary signs that the capacity is becoming yours: you can do one protective act without the helper present; disagree with them without losing your ground; recover after missing a practice; ask for help without handing over judgment; and leave a relationship or method that starts demanding obedience. Don’t wait until you feel completely adult. Once you can carry five percent, test that five percent in daily life.

If the word parent summons the wrong archetype, use safe adult, guardian, advocate, older sibling, wise aunt, good host, captain, or future self. The label matters less than the functions: warmth, protection, and direction.

The Three Adult Functions
I call the deeper therapy pl/ork: play + work. Calling it only “work” forgets the inner child, who also wants to play.

Most people are stuck identifying as the inner child, which can leave them wandering helplessly through adult life from the place that feels small, frightened, abandoned, impulsive, or helpless. Reparenting begins when the present-day person occupies an adult position and cares for the child from there.

These are three functions of one adult, not three separate inner people. One act can carry more than one function.

Nurturer: receives pain with warmth. “It is okay to feel this. You do not have to perform, solve it, or reassure me. I am here.” The Nurturer makes room for what is true without attacking the person who feels it. The Nurturer also takes pleasure in the child’s company: “I’m glad you’re here.” The child needs to be enjoyed, not only comforted when hurt or managed when difficult.

Protector: makes the warmth believable through boundaries, competence, and ordinary action. The Protector works outside and inside: locking the door, ending the conversation, making the appointment, and also preventing a surge of fear, rage, craving, or shame from controlling speech, messages, spending, substances, sex, or relationship decisions.

Leader, Guide, or Guru: holds standards, chooses direction, and helps the child grow toward a whole life. The Guide does not need certainty. It chooses the next sane, value-consistent action while the future remains unknown. I consider this the most important and most easily misread function.

Worth and adult capacity are different. The child did not become worthy when a reliable adult finally appeared. Worth was never the missing skill. What may be missing or inaccessible are adult capacities: pausing, regulating, protecting, choosing, staying, repairing, and acting consistently. Reparenting does not manufacture a better human being. It builds reliable functions around a life that was already worthy of care.

The adult position keeps the child inside the relationship. The healed child carries qualities adults often lose: intrinsic and unconditional worth, vivacity, cuteness, silliness, irreverence, rawness, creativity, originality, innocent love and trust, intuition, and an unexplained connection to the Great Mystery before a chosen path gives it a name.

The healed adult draws from the child and gives back in a continuing loop of love and learning; the relationship is communion rather than command. The main council is internal. Reach outside to loved ones, peer counseling, or a therapist when something is bigger than you and your parts can hold alone.

Intuition and analysis are different instruments. Intuition can register patterns and gestalt before the analytical mind can explain them; analysis checks contradiction, track record, and whether the conclusion actually follows. The analytical mind can also get tired of carrying uncertainty and be relieved to hand that job away. Neither gets unilateral control. A strong intuitive click is information, not proof.

Keep the feeling and test the conclusion against track record, respect for boundaries, independent evidence and contradictions, and what happens when you disagree or say no. A beautiful voice, true teaching, healing experience, or moment of extraordinary attunement can be completely real without making somebody a trustworthy authority in every domain. The test is whether the relationship makes me more grounded, truthful, and free.

Make the Protector Visible
Set one boundary. Say no to something costly. Handle an avoided task or financial mess. Leave a relationship that keeps eroding safety. Keep one small promise. Clean your room. These acts accumulate into trust because internal soothing without external competence can sound like the same unreliability that wounded the child.

Some protective acts happen inside the relationship. Hearing “big whoop” without retaliating, allowing distrust without demanding reassurance, admitting that a complaint is true, and repairing after an internal attack can be as visible to the child as cleaning a room or keeping an appointment. Non-retaliation is evidence of love; it is not a substitute for love.

When the Nurturer is unavailable, the Protector can begin the relationship. Care may first appear as a locked door, a meal, a cancelled obligation, or a phone put down at midnight. Warmth can come later.

Make one chosen act specific: when will you do it, how small can the first step be, and what support or resources does it need? A brief rehearsal may help with a predictable difficult moment. Afterward, review what actually happened. Not doing it may reflect fear, uncertainty about the goal, lack of skill, exhaustion, or a practical barrier; don’t decide which without looking. Completing it without immediate relief is not automatically failure, and completion alone does not prove it helped. Adjust the action to what it actually contributes to safety, connection, competence, or enjoyment—not just to whether it earns a tick.

Love Doesn’t Have to Wait for Trust
Sometimes love is available and still feels unsafe because love and trust have become tied together as though one has to validate the other. They don’t.

The younger state can look at the grown-up life and say, “I don’t trust you.” The adult can answer, “I believe you. And I love you.” Trust is allowed to examine old promises, continuing self-attack, and failures of protection. Love does not have to wait for trust to issue a favorable verdict.

When I was about five, my mother told me it was time for bed—or something equally outrageous to a five-year-old. I cried and screamed, “I HATE YOU!” She answered, “But I love you!”

That completely threw me. Her answer did argue with the emotional logic of the moment, and that may be exactly why it reached me. She did not tell me I wasn’t angry. She introduced a second truth: my hatred did not determine her love. I could hate somebody who loved me. The relationship did not have to mirror my current emotion to remain real.

Don’t turn the sentence into a formula. “But I love you” can become dismissive when it means, “Therefore stop being angry.” The useful test is whether the love can remain alive, hear the anger, and take whatever is true in it seriously. Non-retaliation is one expression of love; it is not a colder substitute for love.

The younger position may say:

“Big fuckity whoopty doo. I don’t believe you.”

The adult can answer:

“Okay. I believe that you don’t believe me. I still love you. You don’t have to love me back. Tell me what feels unsafe.”

The adult does not have to accept every conclusion. Listen for the concrete complaint without fighting the heat around it. If the criticism identifies something serious, answer it seriously.

A dramatized scene in A Beautiful Day in the Neighborhood illustrates the same move. A cynical reporter suggests that it could not have been easy for Fred Rogers’s children to grow up with him. Rogers pauses, accepts that there may be truth in the criticism, and thanks him for the perspective. He neither collapses under the provocation nor fights it. He answers what is worth answering.

Also notice what happens between the internal positions. If distrust is followed by accusation, the younger state may learn that love remains available only while it is received correctly. The resentful voice and the vow-making voice may be the same position, different positions, or a blend. Don’t merge them simply because both appear through the grown-up mind.

When life has gone badly, the internal system may try to recover control by finding a defendant: some younger version must be guilty for failing to grow into the adult who was supposed to save everybody. But identifying the guilty age does not create the missing capacity.

A five-year-old, an adolescent, a younger adult, and the person you were last year each acted from the awareness, conditioning, fear, support, and options they could actually access then. That does not make every choice harmless. Choices can hurt people, damage a life, and require repair. It means punishment cannot retroactively manufacture the awareness or capacity that was missing.

Instead of asking which version deserves blame, ask:

What was that version trying to accomplish or avoid?

What did they understand at the time?

What options could they actually see and tolerate?

What happened because of the choice?

What capacity, support, or protection was missing?

What can the present adult learn, repair, or do differently now?

“Big fuckity whoopty doo—what are you gonna do for me?” is still a literal question. But the first answer may be relational rather than practical: the love remains even when it is not believed. Trust can then update from both relational and ordinary-life evidence—being heard without retaliation, admitting where a complaint is true, repairing after an internal attack, keeping a boundary, attending an appointment, or handling one neglected problem.

Relaxation may help you remain present for this conversation. It cannot decide whether the complaint is true, assign guilt, or create trust by itself.

Make a Simple Vow
When promises still carry some credibility, a quiet vow can help. You can make it with or without a visualization of the two of you playing on a swing:

“I know you are in there. I love you. You do not have to love me back or trust me yet. I cannot promise that nothing painful will happen, that I will always know what to do, or that I will never react badly. I can promise to tell you the truth, listen when you are angry, make our life safer where I can, take the next protective step I can see, and come back to repair when I fail. You do not have to believe these words. Watch what I do.”

Trust may still answer, “Big whoop.” That is allowed. Choose one response small enough to repeat. It may be practical—keeping an appointment, ending an unsafe exchange, eating, sleeping, or handling one neglected task—or relational: listening without retaliation, admitting where the complaint is true, or repairing after you attack yourself. The vow becomes credible through repetition, not one beautiful session.

You take the adult position again whenever the child appears. Trust grows from consistency, compassion, and action rather than one beautiful session.

Give the Vow a Physical Reminder
You can give the promise a physical place in the room: a childhood toy, familiar stuffed animal, visible self-love note, or another object that feels steady and kind.

Choose what it means while you’re sober and regulated. Let it remind you of the Nurturer or Protector you’re practicing becoming. It doesn’t speak for the child, contain secret wisdom, or become an oracle. Your adult judgment stays in charge.

Pair the object with one ordinary promise. A stuffed animal might sit beside a note saying, “When I see you, I remember to stop attacking myself and go to bed on time.” Symbolic comfort becomes more believable when the Protector follows through.

A calm pet can offer companionship while you settle, but the animal is a living companion rather than a prop. Let them come and go without making them responsible for the session.

When the Adult Voice Feels Fake
This is where many people get stuck. You activate the Nurturer, try to speak to the inner child, and get silence. Or numbness. Or a voice saying, “This is so silly.” Suddenly the phone needs checking, dinner needs planning, and a minor household task becomes emotionally urgent.

My friend Scott said:

“Half the time when I try to connect to baby me, I feel fake and it seems crazy.”

I used to think the answer was mainly persistence: keep showing up and eventually the child will trust you. There’s some truth in that. A child expecting abandonment probably won’t place full confidence in one meditation. Persistence alone still misses what is happening when the whole thing feels fake.

The Parent You Inherited
Before you deliberately build the Nurturer, Protector, and Guide, you usually already have another inner voice: the parent you inherited from how you were treated. It can be warm, but for many people its first version is a critic or warden: don’t cry, don’t be needy, don’t get angry, don’t embarrass us, don’t become too independent. Piece by piece, the child learns which qualities have to disappear from view to preserve belonging.

The inherited parent compares the outward persona with an impossible ideal, then attacks from both sides: you aren’t good enough, and whatever looks good is fake anyway. No wonder the inner child doesn’t immediately answer when a new adult voice appears and says, “Trust me.”

Borrowed adulthood helps because the new voice does not have to win an internal election on the first day. It can arrive as an outside relationship, written plan, or concrete protective act and earn credibility through repetition.

Start With Whatever Showed Up
The cynical voice, numbness, urge to scroll, anger, dissociation, or sudden need to do something else all belong inside the pl/ork. The response may be a protector, the child speaking directly, an inherited critic, a present-day grievance, or a blend. Start with what it is trying to prevent, accomplish, or communicate before deciding who is speaking.

Sometimes it is only a thought. Do not create a new inner person every time the mind produces a sentence. Use the least elaborate model that helps. A thought can be labeled “thinking,” allowed to pass, and followed by a return to the room. If it keeps returning with a coherent fear, strategy, age, or demand, a parts dialogue may help. The model serves the person, not the other way around.

Once you have a tentative sense of what is present, don’t stop at naming the voices. Notice what each response teaches the others. A loving vow followed by blame when it is doubted teaches something very different from the same vow followed by curiosity. A protector who warns and is heard behaves differently from one that has to become louder because nobody listens. Often the useful insight is in the relationship between the positions, not in the label you give each one.

Pushing past the response can confirm that you still don’t listen when part of you says no.

You might say:

“I notice you. I’m not going to force past you. What are you trying to prevent, accomplish, or tell me?”

Then wait. Don’t interrogate the silence. Don’t manufacture an answer because this was supposed to be a productive healing session and you already lit the candle.

Two Common Protective Patterns
Two patterns often do have a protective function. Proactive protection stays on duty most of the time: the skeptic, intellectualizer, inner critic, or voice watching for weakness and self-indulgence. It may have spent years preventing humiliation, deception, overwhelm, or another abandonment. It has probably watched you begin other healing methods with great enthusiasm and then disappear. It may be tired, but it is experienced and competent.

Reactive protection often arrives when pain gets close. You suddenly want to drink, smoke, eat, scroll, text somebody, plan something, fix something, sleep, dissociate, get angry, or act out sexually. The response may be trying to step in because nothing else feels able to hold what is coming up.

Both often grew around an attempt to prevent humiliation, deception, overwhelm, abandonment, or loss of control, even when the current strategy is harmful. Treating the response as an enemy, “block,” or moral failure usually gives it another reason to remain in charge.

A Bottom-Up Sequence
Notice the protective part. Don’t push through it.

Offer it the same Nurturer voice you intended for the child. “I see you. I know you’re trying to protect something. I want to understand.” Borrow that voice from somebody safe when you can’t generate it yourself.

Ask what it expects will happen if it steps back. The answer may be: “This will become another performance, and you’ll lie to yourself again.” Or: “If you really feel that pain, it will destroy you.” Or: “You tried this before. It didn’t work. We can’t survive another disappointment.”

Acknowledge the fear before answering it. The protective part may be describing your history accurately. The Nurturer receives what it has been carrying rather than winning an argument.

Let the Protector build trust through ordinary life. Keep a promise. Set the boundary. Handle the task. Leave the situation that keeps injuring you. Make your actual life safer.

Real-world competence eventually convinces protective parts that a trustworthy adult has arrived. One compassionate internal speech usually won’t outweigh years of contrary evidence.

Only after the protective layer feels heard does the inner child often come forward. Push past the guards and you may produce a convincing performance of inner-child pl/ork—the right words, the right visualization, perhaps even the right tears—while nothing underneath feels safer.

The protector does not have to disappear. It may remain watchful while willingly allowing one small step, provided you remain present and able to stop. If it says no, don’t turn that into permission.

Then follow what changes. A critic may soften into a fear; a vague hurt may become a specific need; anger may clarify a boundary that needs action. Respond to what has become available instead of restarting the original exercise. When a need becomes clear, try offering that kind of care and notice how it lands. You don’t need an age, a recovered memory, or a complete cast of parts to work with a feeling. A useful shift may be small; more intensity is not the only way forward.

When the Urge to Escape Arrives
Sometimes the protective response is a sudden desire for a substance, a relationship, a new project, or anything else that can move attention away from pain. Don’t shame the urge. Notice what it may be signaling: something painful has come close enough to require management. That can be progress.

Thank the protective part. Slow down. Bring in support when the material is bigger than your present capacity.

This is also why I caution against forcing your way into intensive meditation, plant medicine, or aggressive trauma processing before enough Nurturer and Protector capacity exists. Those tools can break through protective layers faster than you can build the ability to hold what comes through.

Listening to the guards is part of the therapy.

Gentle meditation or self-hypnosis can still help. My warning is about intensive versions. Gentle versions can genuinely help to calm the nervous system. Many people find that if there’s a mantra that they feel some heart connection to, repeating that mentally or verbally, especially with a nice melody, can lower the defense walls enough to allow the heart to open up. It’s not something to do all the time as a continuous cope, but it can be super effective when used therapeutically. Sometimes There Isn’t a Clear Child Yet
Reparenting assumes there is a coherent inner child to love. For many people, there is. For others—especially people raised in controlling families, rigid religious environments, or any setting where belonging required performing someone else—the authentic child-self may never have had room to form.

The performed self began so early and became so complete that “What do I believe?” or “What do I want?” has no clear private answer. Asking those questions may have been punished, mocked, or made impossible.

Then “love yourself” can feel empty. Love what? The performed self doesn’t feel real, and the authentic self never received the conditions needed to develop. The pl/ork begins earlier, with finding or building the self.

From Survival to Experimental Play
When childhood is organized around avoiding pain and preserving attachment, attention goes into reading danger, meeting other people’s expectations, and keeping the bond. There is much less room for the ordinary experiments that answer, “What do I like? What do I want to try? Who am I when nobody is grading me?”

Identity grows through trial and error: choosing clothes, hobbies, friends, ideas, and roles, then changing your mind. When curiosity or mistakes bring ridicule, punishment, or withdrawal of love, the performed self gets better at surviving while the less-practiced self remains blurry.

Recovery includes more than uncovering a fully formed “true self” hidden underneath. Some of it is developmental pl/ork you get to do late: creating enough safety for preference, curiosity, and experimentation to finally grow.

Possible signs include:

Self-love practice feels performative; you do the technique and nothing lands.

You know what you want mainly through somebody else’s desire, disapproval, or emotional cues.

Your preferences, beliefs, and feelings change dramatically with the people around you.

You don’t know what you would choose without the fear of disappointing somebody.

“What does the child feel?” produces a guessed answer rather than a felt one.

The early practice is finding out who is actually in there: long reflection without external input, private journaling written without imagining a reader, time alone in nature, and the recovery of buried preferences—foods you really like, activities that feel good, beliefs you hold privately, and feelings that appear when nobody else is supplying the emotional cues.

Focusing can be especially useful because the felt sense may know what the conditioned self was trained to forget. Some people also benefit from temporary, intentional distance long enough for the conditioning to quiet down and authentic preferences to surface.

Reparenting and identity-building can proceed together. For some people the child-self is already easy to feel. For others, creating the conditions for it to emerge is the first phase.

Untangling the Belonging Bargains
Identity formation also requires untangling from family emotional systems, religious orthodoxies, group identities, political tribes, and individual relationships that pull you away from your own knowing in exchange for belonging.

Most social systems ask for some conformity. Families want a particular kind of son, daughter, or sibling. Religions want particular beliefs and behavior. Political tribes expect consistent positions and predictable reactions. Friend groups develop rules about acceptable topics, humor, and emotion. A relationship can require one performance of self to keep the bond intact.

When value isn’t felt internally, a person can become a “human doer”: worth has to be earned in an external arena. It may be beauty, athleticism, or sexual desirability; intelligence and grades; money, status, or a sophisticated spiritual identity; or becoming indispensable to everybody else’s problems. In its controlling form, caretaking can drift into keeping other people dependent, because being needed has become the caretaker’s main source of value.

Beauty, intelligence, achievement, and caretaking can all be real gifts. They become belonging bargains when failure in the arena feels like loss of self.

The unparented child takes those bargains because belonging feels like life or death. Self-differentiation means noticing where belonging is being purchased with self-betrayal and learning to keep your own ground. Differentiation also means noticing when praise, pity, fear, attraction, or resonance suddenly changes what you think you know.

Four Pairs That Commonly Hook the Child
Pema Chödrön gives a quick map for finding the bargain: pleasure and pain, gain and loss, praise and blame, good reputation and disgrace. The child can panic not only when something hurts, but when praise, romance, money, status, belonging, or a spiritual high might disappear.

Ask:

What do I think I am about to gain or lose?

Whose approval suddenly feels like survival?

Am I protecting a real value, or trying to preserve a high?

What would remain worth doing if nobody praised me for it?

The aim is not to stop enjoying pleasure, praise, gain, or belonging. Notice when they have become conditions for having a self.

Differentiation can happen inside family, religion, community, or relationship. Selective belonging still leaves room to belong. A differentiated adult can remain deeply embedded while retaining the freedom to question, disagree, or leave when staying would require disappearing. Families with individuated members are healthier than enmeshed families; autonomous faith is healthier than religious compliance; relationships between differentiated people are sturdier than mutual absorption.

In ordinary life, the movement looks like this:

Other people’s emotions and opinions stop automatically replacing your own.

You notice when you agree in the moment and disagree later—and when praise, attraction, fear, or belonging suddenly changes what you think you know.

You can care about somebody’s feelings without sacrificing your own ground.

You can disappoint people, withstand social pressure, and remain recognizably yourself across settings.

You can love people in a family, religion, relationship, or political group without becoming the group identity, and question what you were programmed to believe while holding the discomfort.

Differentiation is slow and lifelong. Temporary distance may be necessary when a system won’t allow it from inside. Some people return later on different terms. Sometimes the leaving is permanent because returning would mean disappearing again. The differentiated adult learns to tell the difference.

Key developed this identity and differentiation portion of the method, drawing on Bowen’s family-systems work and her own self-therapy.

Let the Child Be Bad at Things
Reflection can reveal what was suppressed, but identity also needs experiments. Try painting, dancing, gardening, a language, a sport, volunteering, music, or something else that has no obvious value to your résumé or social position. Notice what holds your attention when nobody is impressed.

The old internal parent may demand immediate talent. The first awkward attempt becomes evidence that you are stupid, clumsy, or not “really” that kind of person. The adult has to protect the child from that verdict: “You’re allowed to be bad at this. We are finding out whether we enjoy it, not proving our worth.”

Give a new interest enough repetitions to get past the humiliation of being a beginner. You may quit because you genuinely dislike it. You may also discover a part of yourself that never had permission to exist. This is pl/ork in its most literal form: play becomes part of identity formation.

The Inner Guide Comes Later
Key adds the third adult function: the Leader, Guide, or Guru. A competent parent does more than soothe and shield. The parent chooses education and experience, holds standards, requires difficult things for growth, and exposes the child to art, music, beauty, nature, and the wider world.

Without direction, reparenting can become permissive. The child receives attention but doesn’t develop. Candy, screens, and escape from anything difficult can masquerade as self-love.

The opposite trap is forced adulthood: perfect discipline, no visible needs, and very little joy or closeness. In that case the next adult act may be asking for help, expressing a preference, or letting a safe person see something imperfect—not tightening control again. Judge the strategy as well as the need. Wanting closeness is legitimate; demanding instant replies or punishing someone for needing space is not the same thing. The Guide helps find a workable way to meet the need without erasing either person.

The Guide points toward a belonging that doesn’t require self-betrayal: nature, existence itself, or whatever your tradition recognizes as ultimate—God, the divine, the universe, source, the Three Jewels, the Tao, life. Time in nature and contemplative practice make this more than another idea.

That ground can make social belonging less desperate. You can love people without becoming the group, stay when staying serves life, and leave when staying requires disappearance.

“Guru” carries spiritual weight for some readers and cult-survivor associations for others. Leader, Guide, Wise One, Sage, Inner Teacher, or Director are all fine. The internal Guide points beyond itself toward what is larger than the social and protects the child from mistaking conditional approval for the deepest belonging.

An external guru who demands surrender places themselves where the source should be. But surrender does not always look like pressure. Sometimes it happens from our side. Praise, mirroring, pity, attraction, special attention, or the feeling of being deeply seen can make the child hand over judgment voluntarily.

Some manipulative people are too smart to present themselves as perfect. They present themselves as gifted but wounded, flawed but honest, dangerous but self-aware, so that every warning about them gets absorbed into a story of nuance, compassion, and spiritual maturity. The Protector must notice when compassion for someone starts excusing what your evidence already knows. An admission like “I have honesty issues and I’m working on them” may itself be honest; it is also evidence that an honesty problem currently exists. Self-awareness is not the same as correction.

Love bombing is not just “too much affection.” Ask what the affection is doing to your judgment. If praise, private attention, or specialness makes contrary evidence disappear or suddenly exempts one person from standards you use everywhere else, don’t argue with the feeling. Slow the decision down and verify.

The same goes for humor. A joke can be playful and still carry a factual claim, request, permission, or boundary move. Don’t ban the joke; test consequential content as if it had been said plainly.

Timing is individual
Most people need the Nurturer and Protector first. Direction requires enough stability for the child to be guided rather than spiritually overruled. Activated too early, the Guru becomes spiritual bypass wearing the clothes of direction. Build warmth and safety first. Let direction emerge once there is a coherent child to orient.

Spiritual openings also happen out of order. Somebody may encounter God, nibbāna, ego death, an extraordinary heart opening, or another profound realization long before they have built a reliable inner Nurturer or Protector. The realization may be completely genuine. It may also show them a depth of peace, freedom, or love that they did not previously know was possible.

The integration question comes afterward.

Did what you discovered deepen or widen love? Can that love reach the frightened, ashamed, angry, or abandoned parts of you? Does it make you more truthful, protective, reliable, compassionate, and capable of relationship?

Don’t let mystery outrun love indefinitely.

Simple Practices
A Heart-to-Child Loop
If you can feel even a little warmth, put one hand on the center of your chest, at the heart chakra, and feel for love. Put the other on the solar plexus, just below the chest, and feel for the inner child.

The solar plexus is the most vulnerable part of the torso. One punch there hurts a lot, as I sadly learned while getting beaten up every day at kindergarten recess by a gang organized by my best friend. Can you believe it? So it feels natural to locate the vulnerable inner child in that region.

Imagine love moving from the heart to the child, then receiving love from the child and sending it back to the heart. Let it become a loop rather than a one-way rescue.

If warmth is absent, don’t fake it. Place the hands, notice what is there, and borrow one simple sentence from a safe adult: “I won’t attack you while we sit here.” Non-cruelty is enough for a first session.

Journal With Each Hand
Write vulnerable thoughts and feelings with your non-dominant hand—the clumsy hand whose script already looks like a child wrote it. Answer with the dominant hand as the adult, offering love, protection, and guidance.

This can bypass the silence people hit with speech or visualization. If the protector is what appears, let that part use the non-dominant hand first. Don’t demand the child before the guard has spoken.

One Intention in the Morning, One Review at Night
In the morning, choose one modest intention:

“Today I will notice one hook before I act from it.”

At night, review the day without conducting a trial:

Where did I notice the hook?

Where did it run the sequence anyway?

What small repair or protective act comes next?

What moment of warmth, pleasure, courage, or connection did I actually receive?

Count recognition as progress, even when it arrived afterward. If you interrupted the pattern, notice that too. Repair what can be repaired, renew the intention, and go to sleep.

Just Like Me
Use this only when you are oriented enough that widening the circle does not pull you away from yourself.

Name one feeling plainly: fear, shame, loneliness, craving, grief, anger, or confusion. Then say:

“Other people know this feeling too. Just like me, they want relief. Just like me, they sometimes make it worse while trying to escape it. May all of us find enough support to stay present and not pass the pain forward.”

You can breathe normally and let the wish include yourself first, then anybody else who comes to mind. This is not a demand to forgive an abuser, imagine their childhood, or open contact while you are flooded. It is a way to stop carrying a universal human feeling as proof that you alone are defective.

Use Childhood Photographs Without Interrogating Them
A childhood photograph can help when visualization feels fake. You don’t have to invent the child’s face or posture; you can look at an image from that time. Look gently and notice what arises without deciding what the picture is supposed to prove.

Useful questions include:

What do I notice in my body?

What feeling, impulse, or association arises?

What does the younger part seem to want me to understand?

What do I remember directly?

What was I told later by somebody else?

What am I inferring from the photograph?

What may be an image, dream, metaphor, altered-state impression, or uncertainty?

What can the adult do for the younger part now?

Don’t ask, “What abuse must have happened?” “Who did this to me?” or “What memory is my body hiding?” Don’t keep questioning one photograph until a story finally appears. That caution isn’t theoretical: guided imagery and suggestive statements can produce false autobiographical beliefs or memories, and doctored childhood photographs have produced them in experiments. A photograph records one visible moment, chosen and framed by somebody; it doesn’t reveal everything outside the frame or explain why a child looked a certain way.

Emotional truth and historical fact are different questions. A photograph, bodily reaction, dream, hypnotic image, or entheogenic vision may show something important about your present emotional world without proving that its apparent story literally happened.

Keep the sources separate: direct memory, something another person told you, inference, image, metaphor, dream, and uncertainty. If a serious allegation begins to form, don’t confront or accuse somebody based only on material produced in this practice. Write down exactly what arose and how it arose, then seek grounded support from somebody who won’t lead you toward a preferred answer.

Put the photograph away when the practice is done. If it starts pulling you into compulsive searching, certainty, or dysregulation, stop using it for now and return to present-day regulation.

Close the Session and Return to Life
Give every inner-child session a definite ending. Endless processing can become another way to avoid living.

Look around and name where you are.

Feel your feet, the chair, or another external contact point.

Relax your jaw and hands.

Name one ordinary action that comes next.

Say: “That is enough for today. We are returning to the life we are building.”

The session does not have to resolve every feeling before you make food, answer a message, take a walk, or go to sleep. Returning to life is part of integration.

When Love Still Feels Missing
If you weren’t well-loved as a child, “love your inner child” may sound like nonsense. You may sincerely want the best for the child, hug them in imagination, or miss them—and still feel none of the beautiful warmth people mean by love.

Begin with smaller recognitions:

Love may already be familiar in another form—or only imaginable as a direction. If care for a friend, child, animal, place, or beauty feels real, borrow from that. If nobody usable comes to mind, imagine somebody whose intention is simply to take good care of you: an ideal adult, fictional figure, future self, or other caring presence. If even that increases threat or loneliness, begin with the least cruel response a decent adult could offer. The exercise is a scaffold, not a test of whether you were loved enough.

Love is also practice. Sleep enough, set a boundary, stop speaking to yourself cruelly, and choose what is good for you. You can act lovingly before the feeling arrives. The Protector can carry part of the Nurturer’s role while the Nurturer comes online.

Begin with the absence of cruelty. Stop the self-attack, shame, and impossible standards. Removing hostility creates room for warmth.

Witness the logic of care wherever you can find it. Therapy, friendship, community, books, stories, fictional characters, and imagined caregivers can model warmth, protection, and repair until you begin to internalize those functions.

What people most often report helping is surrendering defenses. For somebody who has never felt safe in love, the ego can function like a rigid security guard. Vulnerability is the opening the feeling needs.

Meeting the protector is one route: ask the cynical or cold part what it is preventing and let it step back by choice. The warmth may be eclipsed rather than absent. Two other approaches come up repeatedly in spiritual and trauma-recovery communities.

Notice the Part That Came Here
Some people don’t merely say that love feels inaccessible. They say, “No. I hate myself.”

I don’t want to argue you out of that. If hatred is what you find inside, it deserves an honest hearing.

But I also don’t want to overlook something else.

If you chose to open this article, come to therapy, ask somebody for help, or try again after deciding you were hopeless, something in you wanted something to be different.

Maybe it is hope. Maybe desperation. Curiosity. Fear. Duty. A refusal to keep suffering this way. Maybe it is love. We don’t have to decide.

Ask that part: “What were you hoping might happen when you brought me here?”

Follow that thread.

Somatic Heart Opening
Rumi says you must keep breaking your heart until it stays open. Some people find that deep grieving gives way to warmth, connection, or a bodily sense of being held by the universe. That describes an experience, not evidence for one particular physiological mechanism. Love can also open grief in the other direction: receiving care may bring sadness for the care that was missing. That need not mean the love failed. Stay with it when you can remain present and retain choice; more tears are not, by themselves, proof of more healing.

Deep primal crying can also overwhelm or retraumatize, especially without support or when a person becomes completely identified with the sadness. Slow down when the body is losing capacity rather than gaining it.


How Much Happiness Do You Know Is Possible?
I think love basically means wanting happiness for someone. That means the horizon of love is partly shaped by the horizon of happiness you know is possible.

If the happiness you know is food, safety, affection, comfort, friendship, meaningful work, and practical help, wanting those things for somebody is love. Babies already understand some of this. Practical love matters enormously.

But a profound spiritual experience can reveal a kind of happiness, peace, freedom, or unconditional love that you did not previously know existed. Then “I want you to be happy” can suddenly mean something much larger. The imaginable destination has changed.

This is one reason loving-kindness meditation can sound almost comically simple to somebody who has never encountered the territory it is pointing toward, while the same practice can become extremely powerful for somebody who already knows that territory. Repeating “may you be happy” is not automatically profound love. Metta is one way of discovering, cultivating, stabilizing, and extending love.

Not everybody needs to go looking for something deeper. If the love and meaning in your life genuinely feel like enough and you aren’t curious about anything beyond them, spirituality does not need to become another self-improvement assignment.

But if you are curious, follow the curiosity rather than pretending to believe. Read people from different traditions. Meet people whose lives make you wonder what they found. Pay attention to the fruits. Does their path seem to make them more loving, peaceful, honest, grounded, courageous, and capable of facing suffering—or mainly more certain that they have the right explanation?

Sometimes the problem isn’t that care is absent. The whole available horizon of life feels too small. A meal, a boundary, a cleaned room, or one kind sentence may help without answering the question underneath: “Why should I want this life at all?”

Take that question seriously. What would have to be possible for existence to feel worth discovering rather than merely enduring?

Curiosity can be enough: “Other people say they have experienced a depth of love, freedom, peace, or connection that I have never experienced. I don’t know whether I believe their explanation. But what if the experience itself is possible for me?”

Don’t assume that the limits of what you have experienced so far are the limits of what is possible.


If You Want to Kill Yourself
When somebody tells me they want to kill themselves, I get stricter about spirituality, not looser.

If you’re about to act, can’t stop yourself, are badly disoriented or intoxicated, or have the means in front of you, first get another human being involved and put distance between yourself and the means. That isn’t my answer to whether life is worth living. It keeps the question open long enough to investigate it.

Before getting philosophical, make sure there is an adult in the room. If the part that wants to die feels like all of you, use the Witness or borrowed-adult move from earlier. Invite any part that can observe what is happening, protect the body, or postpone an irreversible decision. If you can already observe the suicidal state without being completely identified with it, skip this step.

Then ask:

What is the self that you want to kill?

Do you think that self is the same thing as your body? Why? Do you have the same body in dreams? What exactly do you expect bodily death to accomplish? If the problem is a mind-state—self-hatred, despair, shame, loneliness—what makes you certain that destroying the body ends it?

Buddhism does not teach that death is an off-switch, nor a new-age return-to-source, free get-out-of-jail card. It teaches that repeated thought and intention incline the mind, that this causal process continues through death and rebirth, that hell realms are possible, and that a human birth is a rare opportunity to understand dukkha (suffering) and bring its causes to an end.

You don’t have to believe that. But if you’re about to bet your life on the opposite claim, investigate it first. Near Death Experiences (NDEs) and seriously studied rebirth reports can’t give reliable odds about completed death, but they can challenge the certainty that destroying the body guarantees nothingness.

The basic mental momentum is visible even within this life. Rumination reinforces rumination. Isolation can deepen despair. Repeated self-hatred makes the mind increasingly practiced at self-hatred. Physical life also keeps giving you obstacles and redirections: other people, sensory reality, responsibilities, practice, and access to help. If the many religious and spiritual traditions and accumulating scientific evidence is right that the causal process of mental existence continues beyond bodily death, killing the body may remove some of those redirections rather than remove the suffering.

My father used to tell suicidal people:

“Oh, you want to kill yourself? That’s wonderful. Now you can change what you’ve always been too afraid to change in your life, and then see if you still want to end it!”

His point wasn’t that dying is wonderful. If losing everything is already on the table, what supposedly impossible change is still too frightening? A relationship? A job? A home? An identity? An obligation? A belief? A role?

Try the reversible changes before the irreversible one. Investigate the self, death, rebirth, love, and the life you’ve been afraid to change while you still have a human body and other people available to help redirect the path.

This human life is the most precious opportunity
As I mentioned in my nibbana article, I’ve had my most profound spiritual peak experiences and realizations when I had the least interest in continued existence. And the Buddha taught in MN 129, attaining a human birth is as rare and precious as it would be for a one-eyed turtle to pop his head up through a hoop on the surface of the vast ocean. You won the cosmic jackpot, so don’t throw away the ticket before you open the envelope to find it!




Altered States Can Deepen the Therapy
Learn to do inner-child therapy sober, in your ordinary mindstate. When you’re ready, my self-hypnosis guide or lucid-dreaming guide can take it deeper. Low doses of entheogens can also synergize: cactus, CBD tea with sublingual mapacho, Loveyhuasca, iboga, and others.

The gate matters. Altered states can get past the guards before the adult functions are ready to hold what appears. Build sober Nurturer and Protector capacity first, or borrow those capacities from grounded support that does not seize authority over the experience.

A past extraordinary opening can also become a reference point without becoming something you have to recreate. If an altered state once showed you a depth of love or wellbeing you had never imagined, that matters. But “I experienced it once,” “I can access it only in a particular state,” and “I can contact it reliably in ordinary life” are three different things.

The beautiful state can become a hook too. A psychedelic opening, dream, hypnosis session, meditation high, or sudden flood of love can become the new standard, and ordinary life then feels like failure. Intensity, relief, beauty, or a sense of revelation does not prove historical truth or completed healing. Look for what becomes more honest, protective, and reliable after the state ends.

If the child cannot receive the love, don’t answer by escalating the intensity or trying to force another breakthrough. Meet whatever is preventing the transfer.

Altered States Can Require Reparenting
Have you ever been attacked by a literal demon? Maybe during sleep paralysis or meditation or on a psychedelic trip? A lot of modern folk laugh at this, yet it’s really quite a common experience. I was being psychically attacked by ghosts in my home as a child. Because I was literally a child, I was an easy target. And since most people never actually grew up, they are easy targets for every kind of influence, including spirit influence.

Those ghosts knew my weakness: dares. So they would dare me to jump out the window. I wasn’t suicidal, but I hated to seem like a “wussy.” Luckily, I trusted my parents with this information, and instead of taking me to a psychiatrist and pumping me full of anti-psychotic drugs, they put bars on the windows. Many kids are not so lucky in modern societies. They may get pumped full of anti-psychotic drugs for the rest of their life once diagnosed. That said, professional assessment may be warranted in some situations. I still played around with half-hanging out the windows, but I wasn’t quite dumb enough to jump out, and it meant something that my parents took it seriously.

As a semi-adult, age 26, I discovered meditation. After I began meditating deeply for a while, it seemed like my astral portal opened. I started getting attacked by spirits during sleep paralysis. They would sit on me, prevent me from breathing, and instill tremendous fear into the core of my being.

Jinn Al Kaboos – that attacks you while sleeping


Yep. Exactly like that.
After multiple episodes of this I decided to stop giving in to the victim mentality, and to start taking it seriously as a part of spiritual practice. My inner guide saw it as a perfect opportunity to demonstrate the power of love over fear. The next time it happened, despite how scary it was, I sent unconditional love to this demon on me. It took some minutes, but the demon left, and it never returned.

I’ve also trained other people to do this during spiritual attack while on psychedelics. It works a lot better if you are already trained in loving-kindness meditation to the point where it’s natural. I’d say it works way better than exorcism techniques (like Julian Palmer’s torping, which he also admits doesn’t work as well as love).

The astral plane is a bit different from the 3D. In the astral realm, unconditional love is a true, ultimately powerful physical force. Like a river, or a moat. There’s no evil that can trespass that moat. But don’t assume it will always work like that in the physical plane. It definitely has worked for me sometimes when facing some thugs, but definitely not with others. Loving someone does not mean you leave the front door unlocked with a sign saying “take whatever you want.”

If you’re not able yet (or in the moment) to summon real love for an enemy, you can at least send love to your frightened inner child from your adult nurturer. Many people also find they can borrow support from God, Jesus, angels, devas, or whatever loving spiritual presence fits their tradition. If I were to do that, I would still be framing it as “Please help this situation with love” rather than “Strike down the enemy!”

This can work like borrowed adulthood. At first, you may need to feel protected or loved by something larger than you before you can access that love yourself. Then you can begin internalizing that power you’re borrowing. Move some of that love down to the frightened child and then, when possible, toward whatever you are afraid of. Continuing to relate to benevolent spiritual powers/beings is still perfectly fine, but they would surely be happy if they rubbed off on you!

If a voice or urge is directing you towards harm, and you feel at risk of acting on it, as with myself and those ghosts at home, move away from that open window and get some outside support. Know the limit of your capacity, but don’t turn it into a prison for life.

Non-altered states can feel like this too
Even if you’ve never literally been attacked by a demon that looked like the one above, the truth is that in this world, we’re always being manipulated and attacked by whatever evil force sees us as an easy target. Even social media algorithms will turn your life into hell if you don’t have the inner parent developed. When you do, social media actually becomes useful and feels nice to interact with now and then.

Many people think reparenting sounds silly, or like the kind of ridiculous therapy people do when they have too much time or money. But you know what’s even sillier? Wandering the world aimlessly and getting manipulated by all kinds of evil forces. 💀


When the Practice Feels Real but Stays Shallow
Some people have the opposite problem: they believe they’re connecting when the contact is staying near the surface. Meditating for hours and then sleeping—especially after you have already slept once—can sometimes open much more emotionally direct material.

What showed me that the daytime pl/ork had stayed shallow was the difference at night. The dream carried far more emotional intensity and unmet need than the conscious session had reached. That contrast was information about the earlier session. It did not make every image or apparent event in the dream a recovered historical fact; dreams and deep states can be symbolic, composite, mistaken, or impossible to verify.

How to Forgive Without Forgetting
For many people, healing eventually includes some form of forgiveness or release from active resentment. It can’t be required on schedule, and it isn’t a prerequisite for safety, accountability, boundaries, justice, or recovery.

Active resentment can narrow what you see and keep the child organized around the injury. Pressure to forgive early creates an enlightened performance while the wound goes underground.

The understanding that helps me is seeing how a person’s conditioning, history, pain, and capacity shaped the harmful move they made. Given the total person they had become by that moment, the harmful move was the only one their accumulated nature had available. Hurt people often pass forward pain they never processed. When that becomes something you can actually see rather than a moral instruction, forgiveness may arise by itself, and the rage that held the person as uniquely culpable can soften into something more accurate.

That does not mean the action was harmless or that no different option existed in the abstract. It means punishment cannot retroactively create the awareness, support, or capacity that was not available enough to guide the choice. The useful work is to repair what can be repaired and make a different response more available now.

Accountability remains. The harm is real. Boundaries, legal consequences, moral judgment, distance, and permanent no-contact may all remain appropriate. You can understand the conditioning, hold the person responsible, never speak to them again, and forgive them.

Understanding somebody’s limitations should make my expectations more realistic, not make those limitations less consequential. “I understand how you became this way” can sit beside “I now understand why I shouldn’t rely on you for this.”

Self-forgiveness uses the same frame, and difficulty forgiving yourself can also make forgiveness of other people harder. Name what you did without taking responsibility for what wasn’t yours. Let remorse register that the action violated your values or caused harm. Remorse can guide repair and changed behavior without turning the person who acted into a permanent criminal. Then move through four steps:

Responsibility: name your action and its impact.

Remorse: let the misalignment with your values register.

Rectify: apologize, make restitution, change the behavior, or prevent repetition where possible.

Release: stop holding yourself hostage after the responsibility has been faced.

The practice is inquiry: How did this person become capable of the harm? What was their child carrying? What moved through them when they hurt you? These questions don’t excuse the action. They make the causal chain visible.

Some harms take years. Premature forgiveness is one trap; permanent residence inside resentment is another. Don’t push or bypass. Return to the inquiry over time, without forcing it or endlessly rehearsing the same case, until the understanding becomes real enough to change the emotional relationship.

When One Life Has Ended and the Next Hasn’t Begun
The inner child often becomes loudest in the gap after one life has ended and before the next has formed: after a breakup, migration, illness, loss of faith, loss of a role, a child leaving home, or the decision to leave a family or community on different terms.

Tibetan Buddhism calls an in-between state a bardo. You do not have to accept its teachings about death or rebirth to use the ordinary-life insight: endings create a period with no familiar identity and no clear replacement.

Ask:

What has actually ended?

What am I still trying to make continue?

What is not known yet?

What does the child fear will happen in the gap?

What practical act or small ritual would mark the ending without pretending I know the next beginning?

Do not force a new identity because uncertainty feels unbearable. The adult can keep routines, protect the body, grieve what ended, and remain with the child while the next life takes shape.

Borrowed Adulthood in Relationship
Reparenting is usually framed as something you do alone or with a therapist. The child was wounded in relationship, though, and a relational injury can only heal so far in isolation.

Hearthwork, my peer-counseling framework, begins: Equal time. Attention without interpretation, advice, or fixing. You speak. I listen. Then we switch. Presence, Warmth, Discipline, Confidentiality. It creates a third kind of relationship alongside ordinary friendship and therapy, one the inner child may need and modern life rarely provides.

The three adult functions translate outward. A good peer can lend Nurturer through presence and warmth and Protector through confidentiality, time boundaries, and respect for “no.” The peer does not supply your Guide or decide what the experience means. A carefully timed question can help your own Guide become audible without interpretation, advice, or authority over the answer. Then the roles switch.

This can be borrowed adulthood in its healthiest form. The peer does not become your permanent parent. They demonstrate a capacity, help you participate in it, and return it to you. Afterward, name what you received and do one piece for yourself.

Therapy remains useful for what therapy does. Community and disciplined peer support do something different. A serious healing path can include both.

Community can work in both directions.

Sometimes loving people give somebody enough safety and belonging for a deeper opening to become possible. Other times the opening comes first. A person discovers a depth of love, meaning, or connection they could not previously imagine, and that changes what kinds of people and relationships they can “manifest” in their life.

Then community helps make the opening practical, real, down to earth, embodied, and so forth.

This is part of why I connect inner-child pl/ork with Hearthwork and with building community. The point is not merely to have a beautiful internal experience. Love moves from spiritual experience to the child, from the child into the adult relationship, from the adult into other people, and back again.

The direction is not always the same. The loop matters more than which door you entered through.

You Are Worthy of Love. Always.
Sometimes self-love begins as warmth. Sometimes it begins as borrowed care, a boundary, a meal, a phone put down, or one sentence you are willing to repeat without believing yet.

You don’t have to feel like a parent before taking the first adult action. The role can begin outside you, become shared, and then become yours. Small, consistent acts of care and protection accumulate into deep healing even when the feeling of love arrives slowly.

Be patient and gentle. You are more than your pain and your past. You aren’t alone, help exists, and you belong to life, nature, and the whole universe. Love precedes anything you could earn. Trust is different. It is allowed to grow slowly from truth, protection, repair, and what you repeatedly do. You do not have to withhold love until trust catches up. The child remains alive inside the adult relationship, bringing creativity, tenderness, irreverence, and wonder. They finally have somebody reliable beside them.

Appendix
How This Relates to IFS
Internal Family Systems, or IFS, overlaps with this guide. It recognizes protective parts and teaches people to approach them without shame or combat. The “meet the protector first” sequence is compatible with IFS.

My reparenting method diverges from IFS at an important point. Standard IFS treats the healer as the Self, a calm and compassionate inner source assumed to be available once parts “unblend.” I emphasize the inner adult you build and practice occupying: Nurturer, Protector, and Leader, Guide, or Guru.

The borrowed-adulthood phase sharpens the difference. When the calm inner source cannot be reached, the person can borrow one adult function from a relationship, image, plan, or action and gradually internalize it.

I have no use for another neo-Advaita, spiritual-bypassing claim that a perfect Higher Self is already waiting underneath everything. A trustworthy adult function is built through care, boundaries, action, and direction.

IFS calls these wounded young parts “exiles.” I find that pathologizing. I prefer to speak of a child who needs parenting and integration, rather than naming them by their wounded or excluded condition.

IFS “unburdening,” often done after witnessing a part’s origin story, can be powerful. It can also become dramatic, memory-focused, suggestive, or destabilizing—especially for people prone to dissociation, DID-like fragmentation, or spiritual bypass.

The expanding map of managers, firefighters, exiles, polarized parts, legacy burdens, Self-energy, guides, and new subparts can also become a maze. Some practitioners use the model gently. Others seem to deepen identity fragmentation while trying to resolve it. Institutionalization doesn’t automatically make a framework clearer, safer, or more true.

A skilled, grounded, non-suggestive, trauma-informed IFS therapist may help, especially when they understand dissociation. For inner-child pain, shame, attachment wounds, self-abandonment, or the absence of a reliable inner adult, a good reparenting therapist may fit better. Understand both the strengths and pitfalls before choosing.
