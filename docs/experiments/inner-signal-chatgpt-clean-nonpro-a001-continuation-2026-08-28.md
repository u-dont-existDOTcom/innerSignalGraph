### Verbatim assistant response

> What you’re describing sounds less like a problem of **accessing love** and more like a problem of **believing love is safe, credible, and durable**.
>
> That would also explain why relaxation hasn’t touched it very much. Relaxation can reduce physiological activation, but the conflict you’re describing is relational: one part of you is essentially saying, *“I’ve seen what happens to us. Why should I trust you?”* And another part is exhausted and angry, saying, *“I’m trying to save us now—why are you still making this difficult?”*
>
> The phrase that stood out to me was **“big fuckity whoopty doo, what are you gonna do for me.”** That younger part doesn’t sound like it needs a more convincing speech about protection. It sounds deeply unimpressed by promises.
>
> And maybe reasonably so.
>
> There’s a strange trap in some “inner child” work where the adult self is instructed to arrive like a perfect parent: *I’m here now. I’ll protect you. I’ll never abandon you.* But if the younger part has learned that attachment is unreliable, those statements can actually sound enormous and unbelievable. Your adult self is making a **vow**, while the child is asking for **evidence**.
>
> So the work may be much smaller and less sentimental.
>
> Instead of:
>
> > “I am going to protect and nurture you.”
>
> it might be closer to:
>
> > “You don’t have to trust me yet. I understand that I haven’t given you much reason to. I’m not going to make you feel differently. I’ll show you what I do.”
>
> And then you build credibility through ordinary things: leaving a situation when somebody treats you badly; eating when you’re hungry; going to sleep instead of punishing yourself by staying up; saying no when you mean no; letting disappointment be disappointment instead of turning it into self-contempt; returning after you’ve emotionally disappeared instead of pretending you never left.
>
> That is a very different relationship from trying to make the child relax.
>
> There’s also something important in the resentment you described: *“you’re the one who was the child and you had the opportunity to grow up.”* I wonder whether those two versions of you have gotten assigned the wrong responsibilities.
>
> A child cannot really be responsible for producing the adult they eventually become. Children adapt. They improvise with whatever emotional, cognitive, and relational resources they have at the time. Some of those adaptations later become miserable ways of living, but they weren't a childhood failure to successfully self-parent.
>
> So your younger self may hear, underneath the adult resentment:
>
> *“You ruined my life and now I have to clean it up.”*
>
> And if that is even partly what the child hears, then of course nurturing from the same voice feels unsafe. It is receiving affection from someone who is also quietly prosecuting it.
>
> That doesn't mean you need to manufacture tenderness toward that younger part. In fact, forcing tenderness may make the whole thing more artificial. A more realistic first goal might be **ceasefire rather than love**.
>
> Something like:
>
> *I’m angry about what happened to us. Sometimes I mistakenly make you the person I’m angry at. You were there, so you become an easy target. I don’t know how to feel warmly toward you all the time. But I’m going to stop demanding that you repair the consequences of things you didn’t understand.*
>
> That can be enough for a while.
>
> And the younger part doesn't necessarily have to become cooperative, grateful, healed, playful, trusting, or relaxed. It can sit there with its arms crossed and think your entire adult-self project is bullshit. Your job isn't to defeat that skepticism. Your job is to become **predictable enough that skepticism gradually becomes unnecessary**.
>
> So perhaps the question isn't, “How do I make the inner child feel loved?”
>
> It might be:
>
> **“What would I have to repeatedly do before this part of me would have good reason to believe I’m on its side?”**
>
> That strikes me as much closer to the difficulty you’re describing.
