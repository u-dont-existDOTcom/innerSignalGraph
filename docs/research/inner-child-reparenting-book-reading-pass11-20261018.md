## Findings F184–F202

### Chapter 3 — Step 1: Discovering your modes (printed 63–88)

**F184 — Formal discovery phase is long but conditional.** The book proposes approximately **4–7 weeks** to identify repeated difficulties, elicit a developmental history, fill in YSQ-S3/SMI questionnaires, use optional-seeming visualization/chair dialogue, and build a personalized mode diagram. Its client examples include one person working with a friend and two with therapists. The purpose is to understand how modes operate before changing them. This is a specific protocol sequence, not proof every helper should postpone a needed meal, exit, shelter, medical visit or present-day boundary.

**F185 — A key limitation of childhood-causation inference is exposed by the Martin example.** His childhood memories initially seem generally positive, yet his therapist presumes a childhood lack of autonomy must account for present overaccommodation. The possible formulation may be clinically useful, but the mere fact of a present behavioral pattern does not **establish** a particular unmet childhood need. The guide's instruction not to force an old explanation when current conditions suffice is an important epistemic safeguard.

**F186 — Questionnaires are supplemental, and private.** The book explicitly calls the YSQ/SMI only one source and lets readers control who sees results. This is a correct limitation. It is not a mandate to diagnose a person from a score or to generate an internal mode for each item. The owner guide avoids required inventories, but there is no need to accuse the workbook of making them definitive.

**F187 — The Step 1 exercise deliberately “floats back” from recent emotion into childhood imagery.** In printed pp.70–74, the person recalls a recent upsetting interaction, holds onto the felt state, and waits for an earlier scene. The pictured childhood event may be emotionally informative, but the exercise cannot independently prove the episode occurred precisely as imagined or caused the present behavior. The dated owner guide explicitly distinguishes felt meaning from historical fact; do not import a suggestive memory-confirmation script.

**F188 — The chair work is a teaching model, not necessarily literal dissociated people.** Printed pp.74–77 suggest switching seats for modes to clarify a critical demand, a tired vulnerable part and a practical adult choice. The cases show that the person can discover a real need for rest amid work pressures. Such sessions may help some readers, but the guide's “least elaborate model that helps” avoids creating extra identities for every thought.

**F189 — Figures 3.1–3.3 show multimodal interaction, not a validated diagnostic algorithm.** The three diagrams place Healthy Adult and Happy Child separately and connect punitive/demanding voices, protective surrender/avoidance/inversion and feelings of vulnerability. The diagrams give named fictional people's examples, not empirically identified maps of anyone else's mind. The owner guide's three adult functions are a lower-complexity alternative representation of substantially familiar jobs.

**F190 — The first-stage summary explicitly favors mode analysis over immediate problem-solving, but the book itself provides a safety exception.** The Step 1 Overview table (printed pp.87–88) tells therapists to resist the temptation to start solving acute problems and keep identifying modes. However, the narrative **on printed p.83** creates a concrete crisis plan for Nora, including personal calming measures, outside help and therapist contact when risk of harm arises. It is therefore valid to identify a *sequencing tension*, not to claim Schema Therapy prohibits crisis support. The owner's version prioritizes immediate present protection and makes the person's own protective action part of reparenting, without requiring a weeks-long mode identification period first.

**F191 — “Don't advise him” may protect autonomy or miss a real practical need.** In printed pp.81–82, Martin becomes frustrated that therapy doesn't provide direct advice; the therapist reasonably worries about reinforcing dependent decision-making and offers gradual transfer. Yet this cannot be generalized into refusing concrete help to people facing an urgent social, access, disability or medical barrier. The guide explicitly treats borrowing adult capacities and asking for real help as compatible with developing autonomy.

### Chapter 4 — Step 2: Recognizing modes in daily life (printed 89–101)

**F192 — Another dedicated recognition interval precedes formal change.** Chapter 4 recommends approximately **3–6 weeks** of daily situations/translating reactions into the person's named modes, and explicitly says people may feel worse as suppressed feelings become more available. That is a program design choice; a reader's worsening is not automatic proof of progress. The guide's “When to Change the Strategy” calls for observing real effects, functioning and voluntary continuation.

**F193 — The workbook itself acknowledges that a therapist pushing aside coping modes can be problematic.** Printed p.101's Step 2 Overview includes client discomfort with the therapist's insistence on pushing a protective mode aside. The prose also validates that protective strategies once reduced danger, and the friend column emphasizes listening without quick judgment. Thus the appropriate critique is that some interventions may exert pressure **within an otherwise empathetic book**; not that the field uniformly demonizes protective defenses.

**F194 — Present facts still matter.** One case describes a friend cancelling due to a medical appointment, and the client's assumption that the friend did not like them. The intervention depends on distinguishing the stated real reason from the feared meaning. Another case considers work overload and recurrent guilt. This strongly matches the guide's evidence/interpretation distinction and checking what a partner or employer actually did before assigning a childhood cause.

**F195 — Daily worksheets can reveal patterns and also risk becoming a new checking ritual.** The recommended ongoing mode diary, chair practice and somatic monitoring may be useful if bounded and linked to concrete changes. But asking a person prone to obsessive analysis to repeatedly identify hidden modes could be counterproductive. The guide expressly warns against holding endless meetings with internal parts. Neither an exercise performed nor a completed worksheet counts as recovery or permanent change by itself.

### Chapter 5 — Step 3: Begin changing modes (printed 102–146)

**F196 — Under one Healthy Adult the workbook includes all three of the owner's adult functions.** Its seven subgoals are to meet the vulnerable child's needs, teach effective expression of anger, limit harmful impulsivity, reduce punitive critics and rigid coping, develop the Healthy Adult and strengthen joyful play. Chapter pp.103–108 explicitly add warmth, real limits, competence, justice/fairness, self-direction, social support, pleasure and sound practical choices. This strongly supports the user's idea that popular reparenting and Schema Therapy overlap rather than differing fundamentally.

**F197 — The self-directed path is present, but the source differentiates severity and support.** In printed pp.102–104, the book says self-reparenting may work for readers with more established Healthy Adult capacity, while more severe problems can benefit from trained therapy and a friend. It does not claim that a lone reader can safely emulate all trauma-scene rescripting described for the professional cases.

**F198 — Image rescripting distinguishes rewriting what a scene means from rewriting historical reality.** Printed pp.113–121 instruct starting with a neutral/pleasant memory, then using a current problem to access difficult childhood emotions, followed by therapist/borrowed adult intervention. Printed p.117 acknowledges factual memory accuracy is not its criterion. The intervention can produce felt safety, but **cannot substantiate newly generated facts, the parent's internal motives, or prenatal memories**. The owner's stronger documentary/source-separation safety rule should be retained. Imagined physical touch by the therapist in a childhood scene is not the same as touching the real patient (book expressly distinguishes them at p.121).

**F199 — Three-part role-play is conceptually useful but can invent parental motivations.** Printed pp.122–129 reverse roles so an adult imagines an old caregiver's fatigue, intention, or explanation, then returns as a child with adult hindsight to express a need. This can loosen global self-blame without the real historical adult changing. However imagined caregiver explanations are interpretations, not evidence of what the historical caregiver truly thought. The author concedes genuinely punitive caregivers may not plausibly respond well and uses a different supportive adult in that case. This matches the guide's realistic expectation and uncertainty safeguards.

**F200 — The critical modes are to be ‘silenced’; the book still permits realistic self-correction.** Printed pp.105–106 and 129–133 make strong, sometimes adversarial chair-dialogue instructions, including telling the punitive mode to leave. But the same pages distinguish a realistic Healthy Adult conscience from attacks on basic worth. The guide similarly stops self-abuse while preferring to understand the response and protect vulnerability without unnecessarily multiplying internal enemies. This is a **method/emphasis difference**, not an argument that Schema Therapy lacks ethical responsibility.

**F201 — Self-inquiry circle adds concrete steps but largely matches the guide.** Figure 5.1 (printed p.137) was inspected visually: “what actually happened,” sensations, thoughts, emotion, interpreted mode, what is needed, what the person did and its actual effect, and what might help next. In the case, actual cancellation for a doctor's appointment is contrasted with an unsupported assumption of personal rejection. A light, optional *facts → feelings → need → small action → outcome* card may be useful, but the dated guide already covers every operation across its existing sections. No separate large workbook module is established as necessary.

**F202 — Late-stage sequencing is not absolutely no-action.** While the Step 3 Overview (printed pp.145–146) cautions therapists not to expect major behavior change immediately, printed pp.138–140 encourage early noticing, a brief timeout for escalating anger, real-world limits and happy/pleasant activities. Printed pp.143–144 show therapist repair and apology following a misattuned appointment change. These **qualify** any description of the workbook as action-free before Step 4. The main difference remains how formally it *stages* change rather than what adult care includes.

## Integrated decision notes

1. **Large overlap confirmed:** these chapters explicitly present the Healthy Adult as caring, protecting, guiding, reality-checking, taking practical action, asking for outside care, holding boundaries, respecting meaningful joy and developing independent choice.
2. **Potential guide additions remain formatting, not a missing theory:** one optional before/after *facts and needs* example from the self-inquiry circle, one three-response example of the same fear (surrender/avoid/invert), a short role-play about a genuine boundary without encouraging recovered-memory certainty, and a modest adult-to-child apology. All need checking against the *current* authoritative owner guide before drafting.
3. **Most substantive disagreement is the permissive early protective action and opt-out boundary:** the workbook stages 4–7 weeks of discovery and 3–6 weeks of recognition before its formal change phase, sometimes presuming childhood roots or describing resistance as coping. The owner's guide allows adults to act in actual safety, call for needed help, decline the entire inner-child method and reconsider rather than repeat nonproductive inward processing. The workbook also includes genuine crisis exceptions; preserve them.
4. **Evidence limits:** fictional/composite cases, therapist dialogues, circle figures and uncontrolled anecdotes do not validate universal treatment outcomes or inferred biographical causes. The PDF's mechanistic statements should be checked against independent evidence *after book reading* where necessary, not substituted for the owner's requested source-based review.
