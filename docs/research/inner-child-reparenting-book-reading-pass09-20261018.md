## New source-based findings F148–F169

### Anthology ch.13 — Schema Therapy for couples (Travis Atkinson and Poul Perris)

**F148 — Couple-to-couple reparenting is explicitly within ST, not new to the owner's guide.** The therapist first models limited reparenting to *both partners*, then helps them become appropriate sources of Nurturer/Healthy Adult care to each other. The authors call a healthy interaction of Healthy Adult, Vulnerable Child and Happy Child the *healthy mode triad*. The clinical goal is self- and mutual responsiveness, not making the therapist a permanent parent. **Disposition:** strong convergence with borrowing adult capacities from relationships and receiving mutual care.

**F149 — A practical connection dialogue is described as an ordered request/response exchange.** Person A describes actual vulnerable feeling, an urge to protect themselves and a specific need; person B recognizes their own defensiveness and responds to the request. In a detailed vignette the therapist prevents both from interrupting, elicits the need to be understood, and helps the listener own their interrupting/defensive reaction. **Potential *format* addition**, if a lay guide needs one example of asking for help without turning partners into obligatory therapists. Not a newly invented clinical component or proven outcome.

**F150 — Conceptual limitation: no automatic “both sides share blame”.** The chapter typically assumes a dyadic mode cycle and says one partner is rarely the sole cause; that formulation is inappropriate as a default when present coercion, threats, violence or exploitation is the operative problem. The owner guide's “Also Look Outward” and protective consent/real-world safety requirements must govern such cases. The illustrative couple worked under therapist supervision with permission before the partner entered imagery; this does not establish safety for every relationship.

**F151 — Partners entering childhood imagery is high-context work.** In the case, the partner receives consent to enter an imagined childhood scene and protest mistreatment while the therapist guides the exchange. It demonstrates how supportive care might be received, **not an unsupervised recommendation to expose personal trauma to an unsafe partner**. The chapter itself concludes that further outcome research on this specific couple model is needed (as of publication).

### Anthology ch.14 — Empathic confrontation (Wendy Behary)

**F152 — Compassion includes accountable limit-setting in this clinical model.** Vignettes involve clients who have betrayed trust or aggressively insulted a therapist: the clinician tries to understand why the defense developed while holding the patient responsible for the actual interpersonal effect and boundaries. **Strong convergence:** the owner's Nurturer (warmth), Protector (safety, stopping harmful conduct), Guide (discernment, repair), and explicit non-harm gate.

**F153 — The therapist reparenting *their own* vulnerable part before responding is part of the text.** The clinician notices feeling personally attacked, pauses and practices an internal compassionate reassurance before returning to a calm limit and encouraging a better exchange. This is a concrete example of self-reparenting as professional behavior, not merely treating the patient. It could be useful in discussion of the helper's responsibility, without requiring a client to imitate a confrontational clinical script.

**F154 — A real tension: strong claims that imagery refusal is always hidden defensive motivation.** The author lists examples of clients who say they cannot visualize, remember, or find the technique helpful, then asserts a concealed reason generally underlies the refusal. The examples **do not rule out** true lack of visual imagery, inadequate method fit, legitimately unwanted trauma work, or sound opposition. Other chapters 4 and 13 respect preferences/permission. **Disposition:** preserve the owner's explicitly final right to decline a technique or the entire frame; do not relabel dissent as resistance by fiat. This is an author-specific overreach rather than a blanket claim about all ST.

### Anthology ch.15 — Authenticity and personal openness (Michiel van Vreeswijk)

**F155 — Clinicians themselves can be performative.** The chapter asks whether seemingly kind therapist statements are sometimes merely technique or conceal anger, discomfort or distraction; dismissing the patient's skepticism as a schema instead of considering therapist inauthenticity is identified as a mistake. Therapist authenticity does not mean indiscriminate self-disclosure. **Convergence** with guide's criticism of therapist/guru infallibility and rejecting automatic pathologization of reasonable objections.

**F156 — Repair examples distinguish admitting responsibility from abandoning boundaries.** Cases include a therapist responding too briefly to a vulnerable email, another inappropriately centering their own bereavement, and co-therapists making an activating announcement just as a group session ends. The clinicians correct themselves and, in the last case, honor the session ending and resume the repair later rather than promising limitless availability. **Potential therapist-selection criterion:** a reliable clinician can name a mistake, apologize, adapt and keep agreed boundaries. Already represented in guide principle.

**F157 — Personal authenticity is not compulsory disclosure.** The chapter explores workplaces forcing expressions of vulnerability, and groups where fake harmonious positivity suppresses hard truth. The author treats both excessive concealment and coerced disclosure as possible problems, emphasizing timing and purpose. **Guide convergence** with privacy, choice and safe/small truthful sharing.

### Anthology ch.16 — Therapist schema activation and self-care (Christina Vallianatou and Tijana Mirović)

**F158 — External structural danger changes the formulation, for *therapists too*.** Authors report how war, displacement, austerity, economic uncertainty and financial crisis affect both clinicians and patients; symptoms cannot responsibly be reduced to childhood-only “Vulnerability” in an objectively unsafe environment. Some socially shared beliefs and survival behaviors are context-dependent. **Strong convergence** with practical external reality checks and finding real resources before excessive psychological interpretation.

**F159 — Caring for others can become self-sacrifice, not reliable parenting.** Therapists may work through breaks, avoid reasonable limits, try to resolve distress immediately, overpromise, or demand perfection from themselves. Authors stress the therapist's own Healthy Adult caring for their “little self”, practical rest, collegial support and culturally realistic self-care rather than adding an impossible regimen. The guide already defines self-protective action as reparenting. **Convergence**, especially caregiver-facing applicability.

### Anthology ch.17 — Compassion-Focused Therapy integration (Olivia Thrift and Chris Irons)

**F160 — Two dimensions of compassion are explicit: engagement with suffering AND skilled action.** The text defines compassion as noticing/approaching distress plus the motivation and ability to relieve/prevent it. Its “Compassionate Self” is guided by **wisdom, strength, commitment** and integrates thinking, bodily grounding, sensory resources, behavior and caring imagery. This is highly aligned with the owner's Nurturer/Protector/Guide, and conclusively rebuts the notion that compassion-based therapy is only soothing without protection or behavior change.

**F161 — “Borrowing” compassion from a trusted example predates the owner's guide.** The case models a compassionate other using a remembered patient teacher, combined with an imagined figure's wisdom and reliable commitment; then gradually transfers those skills to the patient's adult self. The book makes an explicit Buddhist comparison to Bodhicitta/Bodhisattva ideas. It also depicts love and support as a sensory experience: sound, texture, pace and caring gestures. **Convergence, no claim of historical novelty.**

**F162 — Courage/action and reality-based evaluation are part of the clinical intervention.** The case shows naming angry/sad/anxious aspects, learning that another person behaved harmfully while acknowledging the patient's own role, setting boundaries, social exposure and attempting a date despite possible rejection. Reported outcomes are **one example**, not direct experimental validation of each element. The patient's competence does not require guaranteeing future romantic success.

**F163 — The diagrams remain conceptual models, not validated maps of a person's brain.** Fig.17.1 draws interacting threat, drive and affiliative systems; Fig.17.2 differentiates attending to suffering from response modalities (attention, reasoning, behavior, imagery, sensory and feeling); Fig.17.3 maps the patient’s vulnerable and angry child experiences, demand/critic and protective responses. The diagram has useful explanatory value but should not be used to label neurological pathology or infer historical trauma from symptoms. **No new required parts.**

### Anthology ch.18 — Eating-disorder specialist treatment (Anna Oldershaw and Helen Startup)

**F164 — Anorexia adaptation is a separate, speculative-to-developing clinical intervention.** The chapter introduces *SPEAKS*, a five-phase schema/emotion-focused model centered on access to an “emotional self”, with a case showing chair work, deeper grief, healthy adult compassion, and eventual independent action. At publication the translation was under feasibility investigation. No basis to recommend unmonitored ED treatment or claim that learning to feel automatically treats malnutrition or complex eating disorders.

**F165 — An interesting sequencing variation is *late* self-compassion.** In the clinical example, coercing an early “love yourself” performance may fail because a patient cannot yet receive it; after engagement with protected unmet needs, adult self-compassion becomes more believable. Contrast guide's “Borrow the Adult Before You Can Be the Adult,” where actual protective behaviors and borrowed support can begin even when warmth isn't yet felt. These are **alternative routes**, not a universal hierarchy. The chapter explicitly names the Healthy Adult from the beginning even if the compassion feeling develops later.

**F166 — The figures are conceptual, not a direct diagnostic instrument.** Fig.18.1 is a patient-selected toy arrangement depicting felt relations among a distant Healthy Adult, critic, detached protector, submissive part and experiencing child; Fig.18.2 hypothesizes a sequence from secondary shame about eating to shame about self to relational grief/anger. No graph of universally confirmed illness causes or a requirement to name every toy. **Guide fit:** choose minimal useful categories; leave a bodily symptom medically assessable.

### Anthology ch.19 — Brief schema-informed CBT (Stirling Moorey, Suzanne Byrne and Florian Ruths)

**F167 — Schema ideas can be used as selective add-ons to CBT.** The chapter describes a practical bridge for short-course therapists: a schema/mode formulation, supportive therapist stance, limited imagery or chair dialogue and continuing ordinary behavioral activation and thinking skills. It explicitly warns that a full Schema Therapy claim is premature in several disorders compared with established CBT outcomes (as of 2020). **Reparenting can be a component of other therapies**, not necessarily a separate whole school.

**F168 — The “good aunt/uncle” analogy explicitly frames limited reparenting with professional limits.** The clinician may lend dependable care, symbolic messages, a recorded reassurance and opportunities for action while building autonomy. It is not permanent therapist dependence. The clinical case also describes psychiatrist review/medication and ongoing support alongside psychotherapy. **Convergence:** borrowed adulthood and external supports are already well established. Therapy for self-harm is not an unsupervised exercise.

### Anthology ch.20 — Endings and the therapy relationship (Tünde Vanko and Dan Roberts)

**F169 — Therapy is expected to end, but care can be internalized and shared.** The chapter frames termination as a process of attachment, grief, adult self-support and practical planning; patients may recall the therapist, hear recorded messages, keep reminder objects and, in some settings, seek occasional booster sessions. It explicitly says limited reparenting **need not come from one caregiver alone** and collective support or transfer is possible. This directly parallels the owner's “Borrowed Adulthood in Relationship,” support network and adult apprenticeship.

**F170 — Not every ending is client “abandonment schema”.** Some treatment ends for insurance, relocation, pregnancy, illness, bad fit or therapist error, and the authors note that a patient may be reasonably angry with an inappropriate early ending. Honest communication, transfer/other support and acknowledgment of the actual event matter. **Guide fit:** distinguish a real breach or constraint from historical projection; choosing providers should include how endings, referrals and expected availability are handled.

### Van Genderen chapters 1 and 2 opening

**F171 — Workbook explicitly distinguishes independent and therapist-supported routes.** Chapter 1 uses three **fictional composite** longitudinal examples: Sarah changes demanding/perfectionist behavior independently, Martin and Nora receive therapist support in personality-disorder contexts. It prescribes five steps from initial discovery and real-world recognition to intervention, independent action and relapse planning. This is a book's teaching architecture, not direct evidence those particular three people improved in a controlled study. **Disposition:** self-directed reparenting has established clinical-book precedents, but intensity and support requirements vary.

**F172 — Full child-needs breadth is not absent.** Chapter 2 opens with emotional connection/safety, autonomy/identity/competence, expression, play, realistic limits, fairness and a coherent self-and-world picture. Table 2.1 associates these unmet needs with **21 pitfalls** including perceived unfairness, incoherent identity and loss of a meaningful world; these belong to the author's broader schema model, not independently verified diagnostic rules. The owner's guide already covers identity development, life meaning, boundaries and fairness; do not claim new domain missing just because a formal table labels it.

**F173 — One belief can lead to three conflicting coping behaviors.** The workbook's Fig.2.1 traces a triggered pitfall to **surrender, avoidance or inversion** (acting as if the opposite of the belief were true). Its friendship example gives compliance, withdrawal/numbing, or arrogant overstatement as alternative reactions to feared inadequacy. This supplies one **potentially clear reader example** for the guide's protective-parts section, but does not prove all cases of boasting or withdrawal hide the same wound.

**F174 — Childhood adversity is not presented as destiny.** The author identifies temperament and helpful relationships/outside supports as moderating factors, and explicitly allows self-directed work for milder difficulties while recommending trained assistance for more severe cases. This reinforces that outside care and structural safety must remain in the picture. A chapter example where unexplained symptoms are described after medical evaluation is not evidence that psychological work replaces medical assessment.

## Practical dispositions

- **Not new to ST/early reparenting:** three adult functions as psychological jobs; care plus accountable protection; partner/peer/therapist borrowing; internalization; positive play; spiritual/Buddhist influence; genuine choice and therapist repairs (some authors); care after formal therapy ends.
- **Possibly useful *illustrative* additions:** a specific couple *request-and-response* example grounded in safety and reciprocity; a therapist-vetting question about handling refusal and premature termination; a short “surrender, avoid, invert” example from van Genderen's Chapter 2; an optional one-minute grounding practice that distinguishes responding from requiring a feeling. These are examples to test for reader utility, **not missing frameworks**, and must be checked against the current registered guide before proposing edits.
- **Preserve strong safeguards:** abuse and coercion are not presumed symmetrical “mode cycles”; an expressed preference or refusal is not automatically a detached protector; psychological accounts cannot establish hidden intent; imagery is not historical proof; symptoms require normal medical attention; high-intensity trauma or ED techniques require appropriate assessment and professional care.
- **Evidence status:** examples and diagrams are *author material*; as-of-publication efficacy references require separate verification when needed, but further external research must NOT displace still-unread book material.
