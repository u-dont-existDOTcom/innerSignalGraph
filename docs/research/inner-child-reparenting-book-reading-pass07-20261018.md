## Findings F90–F114

### Ch.3: Somatic perspective — actual focus and tensions

**F90 — Body-focused attention is framed as consent-sensitive collaboration, not an emotion detector.** The authors recommend noticing a patient's posture, breathing, gestures and shifts, then asking how the person experiences them. Importantly, they say the therapist must remain open-minded rather than presume what a movement means. The sample client may explore sadness, anger, or a feeling of collapsing, but the patient's reports, not a prescribed body code, establish what the experience is like. **Guide match:** “Catch the Hook Before You Interpret It” and “Regulation May Come Before Dialogue” already separate sensation from conclusion. Preserve that boundary.

**F91 — Respecting a protective 'stop' is explicitly modeled inside clinical ST.** One therapeutic dialogue features a patient saying that further discussion will overwhelm them; the therapist praises the ability to recognize their limit, stops and explores the protective function only with renewed agreement. The patient is not required to remove the protective part. **Guide match:** “A Bottom-Up Sequence,” “Before You Try to Go Deep.” This is substantial overlap, not an owner's unique innovation.

**F92 — 'Window of Tolerance' is operational, but not a universal measure.** Figure 3.1 depicts hyper- and hypoarousal around a middle workable band. The chapter stresses that inward body attention itself can dysregulate some clients, and recommends narrowing attention to one or two dimensions and returning to grounding/other support. This is a *practice model*, not proof that arousal categories can be accurately diagnosed from chat words or that the hypothetical curve is an objective biomarker. **Guide match:** existing duration, recovery, orientation, stopping and consent gates are more explicit on observable checks. No reason to replace them.

**F93 — The chapter's ‘core organisers’ give a helpful but optional map of felt experience:** cognition, emotion, interoception, movement and external sensory perception. Their aim is a flexible, patient-led exploration that starts from whatever is accessible, rather than insisting on a verbal child dialogue. The guide already accepts thoughts, sensation, gesture, writing, and nonverbal experience. Could inform a *single concrete example*, not a new taxonomy requirement.

**F94 — Somatic resources can be borrowed from the person's *already successful habits*.** Rather than prescribe a generic breathing style, the therapist observes a naturally comforting hand gesture, asks whether it helps, and builds on what the client genuinely experiences. This aligns with the guide's practical borrowing of a missing adult function. Specific novel usability possibility: an optional prompt “what are you already doing that helps a little?” whose purpose is to identify a usable resource; do not call it a missing conceptual role.

**F95 — Relational cues, including therapist voice, can soothe OR activate fear.** The chapter explicitly recognizes that a soothing voice, particular facial expression, or eye-contact shift may be associated with threatening prior experiences for a given client. Hence there is no one magical caring tone or touch; adapt to actual response. The guide already says closeness can feel intrusive and advises changing distance, voice or source. **Convergence.**

**F96 — Sensorimotor ‘probes’ are a distinct format worth evaluating as an optional exercise.** Therapist says a brief alternative belief, then patient observes bodily/emotional response without needing to affirm it, agree, or perform positivity. The words and intensity can be altered, or the exercise stopped. It is explicitly **not the same as repeatedly telling oneself an affirmation to make it true**. For readers who say “I know it intellectually, but the words feel false,” such an experiment might clarify the barrier. However the therapist's interpretation of “no response” as evidence of a coping mode is not established without examining other explanations (poor fit, genuine disagreement, fatigue, nonverbal differences, safety). Some neurophysiological claims in the chapter outrun the case-based evidence. **Candidate: optional gentle, consent-based reflective example after comparison with current guide; not an unsupervised diagnostic test or forced affirmation.**

**F97 — Longer positive emotion is not synonymous with deeper healing.** Authors suggest allowing positive contact to settle in, with a slow titration if joy or care feels threatening. This may help illustrate receiving love rather than merely speaking it; owner guide already distinguishes offer/receipt. Claims of automatic new neural pathway formation from staying longer with affect are **authors' proposed mechanism**, not demonstrated efficacy for this practice. Never demand that the person stay in an experience if they want to stop.

### Ch.4: Core needs / seven parenting patterns

**F98 — Limited reparenting is not only consolation.** The seven PPPs are a relational checklist spanning warmth, joyful play, supporting competence, granting personal autonomy, being dependable, intrinsic worth and parent/therapist competence. “Strength and Guidance” is itself nested within Emotional Nurturance and Unconditional Love; it supports practical goal setting and follow-through. The owner's Nurturer, Protector and Guide cover these functions with different packaging. **Historical/practical convergence.**

**F99 — Autonomy Support and Autonomy Granting are genuinely different.** Supporting ability means seeing and encouraging someone's capacity; granting autonomy means allowing choices including privacy, preferences, not being monitored, the right to limit therapy, and a life direction the helper would not select. One case specifically concerns an introverted person's genuine preference for few deep relationships over conventional socializing; another concerns a patient who does not feel anger toward a parent when therapist thinks anger might be expected. **Guide match:** already explicitly warns against using the helper's model to overrule the person. May provide a concise analogy in a future section on choosing a therapist.

**F100 — Disliking imagery can be a true preference, not necessarily a symptomatic defense.** The chapter's case of a patient who finds imagery distracting ultimately treats the preference as reasonable and continues reparenting without imagery. It explicitly admits some clients progress without it. This is a specific literature precedent supporting the owner's frame-level consent principle; avoid claiming every ST practitioner insists on imagery. **No new concept.**

**F101 — The chapter contrasts enjoying the child with treating them as a problem to fix.** A clinician initially focuses only on analyzing a withdrawn little girl's fear; supervisor suggests joining her drawing/play instead. The authors warn against therapist ‘toolbelt mode’ that misses connection and fun. This strongly matches the guide's “child needs to be enjoyed, not only comforted” and child-to-adult reciprocity. **No novelty claim.**

**F102 — Sensitivity/temperament is contextual, not intrinsically deficient.** The authors argue certain personality temperaments may need different forms of care and respond differently to supportive or stressful settings. Their 'orchid/dandelion' metaphor and statistics are attributed to cited research; **this chapter does not itself test an individually matched reparenting protocol**. The guide could use temperament-respect as a therapist-vetting question if not elsewhere; avoid prescribing labels from an anecdote.

**F103 — Dependability includes both emotional availability and competent external follow-through.** The cited “day and night” metaphor is adjusted for the bounded therapeutic relationship, including crisis arrangements when appropriate. The Healthy Adult is to internalize dependability. This parallels the guide's claim that actual appointments, boundaries, and repair make an inner parent credible, and its stance on shared external support. Do not imply clients should demand around-the-clock therapist access.

**F104 — Therapists' ability to learn from disagreement is itself a positive-parenting trait.** “Confidence and Competence” includes staying steady in response to patient challenges and correcting course. This reinforces the guide's anti-guru and helper-correction argument; it is **not a gap in ST theory**.

**F105 — The seven-factor research is correlational and retrospective.** The chapter explicitly notes research was on adults' **recalled parenting** correlated with measures of functioning, not outcomes of actual therapist reparenting using these seven factors. The proposed use as a training checklist remains a plausible clinical extrapolation. Prior Pass 01 correctly separated this from evidence the seven-factor procedure itself is effective. No inflated treatment-claim language.

### Ch.5: Core principles of imagery

**F106 — Imagery has more affective intensity than discussion, and that cuts both ways.** This is an explicit literature-based theoretical point in the chapter: imagined experiences can evoke strong feelings. It is neither a blanket demonstration that intense feelings mean benefit nor that more intense imagery is always appropriate. The owner guide's safe-dosage rule is compatible.

**F107 — The three-step cycle already teaches therapist→Healthy Adult→child receiving care.** Actual sequence: patient describes a childhood scene; therapist or a borrowed helper intervenes and supports; patient's Healthy Adult increasingly assumes the role; child perspective re-experiences care and can request modification. The book is **very close** to the owner's borrowed-adult/apprentice model. It shows that transferring a borrowed parental capacity to the person was not invented in the guide.

**F108 — The source distinguishes imaginal rescripting from a new factual history only implicitly.** It describes explicitly changing an image of what occurred to cultivate different meaning. It does **not** provide a detailed false-memory/provenance framework comparable to the guide's childhood photograph/memory section. The safe distinction is “the *meaning and felt relationship* can change, while the historical event remains what it was.” Never treat images generated during a therapy exercise as recovered factual memories.

**F109 — A positive-image or future-rehearsal route can avoid dramatic trauma scenes.** The chapter uses imagined future interpersonal choices, positive figures (real or imagined), and preparing to act differently now. Your guide already offers borrowing from real/spiritual/imagined helpers, small practical commitments, and short rehearsals. The possible difference is a **worked rehearsal scenario** and feedback mechanism, not the functional idea.

**F110 — Some therapist tips create a tension with autonomy granting.** The chapter proposes introducing imagery spontaneously to reduce anticipation/avoidance in some cases, but also discussing fears and slowing or stopping if dissociation occurs. It would be incorrect to interpret this as permission to bypass informed preference or a firm no. Other ST chapters explicitly permit rejection of imagery. Preserve the distinction between a consenting patient worried about a technique and someone who declines the technique or the framing itself.

### Ch.6: Childhood memory rescripting

**F111 — Not all antagonists receive the same adult intervention.** The chapter distinguishes a punitive abuser (firm protection), a perfectionistic/demanding caregiver (balanced persuasion and protecting play), a guilt-inducing caregiver (uncoupling child's responsibilities from adult's feelings), and an absent/neglectful caregiver (showing up and meeting the child's needs). These concrete examples could improve guide usability but do not require four more inner-part labels. The owner's Protector/Guide/Nurturer can respond differently within their existing single-adult framework.

**F112 — A significant epistemic virtue: imagery need not force the caregiver to transform.** In an example of a neglectful mother, the therapist stops short of inventing remorse and instead provides the child with care even if the historical adult remains unreceptive. Some losses, including death, cannot be “fixed”; the parent function means accompanying grief. This parallels the guide's realistic outcome/boundary stance. **Strong source-grounded convergence**; not proof of actual historical dialogues.

**F113 — Rescripting process explicitly checks what care feels like from the child's side.** The therapist asks whether the image response feels safe/adequate, and adjusts if the patient needs more support or different care. This directly matches the guide's “care offered versus care received” distinction and the right to distrust until repair is demonstrated. **No new concept.**

**F114 — Specific guardrail in clinical use: avoid reliving the worst abuse simply to achieve ‘depth’.** The chapter recommends stopping just before the worst acts, titrating emotion, recognizing flood/hypoarousal, leaving time to debrief and end. Although the therapist seeks enough meaningful contact for rescripting, it is not an instruction for readers to generate maximal distress. The owner guide's stronger external orientation/stop/recovery criteria remain appropriate. **No direct unsupervised trauma protocol transfer.**

### Ch.7: Trauma and complex PTSD — scope, diagrams and consent

**F115 — Trauma mapping separates incident, response, and meaning.** Figure 7.1 shows several remembered events as distinct circles, each divided into event, affect and meaning, connected where themes recur. It does not show what caused every life difficulty and does not establish memory truth. A present-day problem can be partly external; historical links remain provisional. The guide already separates observation, emotional response, interpretation and practical safety. **Possible optional simple example**, not a new childhood-memory investigation mandate.

**F116 — A second diagram captures a goal-directed practical loop.** Fig.7.2 connects a negative meaning/belief and emotion to three coping responses—avoidance, surrender, counterattack—interfering with the Healthy Adult's goals of connection, autonomy and responsibility. This complements the already-proposed single-fear/multiple-response illustration. In the clinical case, some real external criticism and coercion were present. Therefore a reader-facing example should keep current harm separate from an *inferred* defense.

**F117 — The IREM chapter describes a trial's methods but not its published results.** The book states the trial outcome data would be published elsewhere. As described here, professionally delivered IR and EMDR were used with childhood-trauma PTSD participants who consented to focused treatment, with therapist protocols and scheduled sessions. The case of “Jane” offers scores and the person's experience but is an **illustrative single patient**, not the randomized comparative result. Earlier research Pass 02 separately verified later published RCT results; this chapter alone cannot be cited for their magnitude.

**F118 — “Without prior stabilization” was about a bounded screened clinical treatment, not a general command to ignore readiness.** The authors still describe preparation, shared formulation, practicing on a less distressing episode, choosing tolerable target memories, relational support, and sensitivity to the individual's tolerance. They explicitly say some people do *not* benefit from immediate trauma treatment if they are unsure about PTSD treatment or do not want trauma memory work. This importantly aligns with the guide's voluntary participation and protective pacing. It also means the existence of a formal pre-trauma stabilization *phase* is a different question from ensuring real safety/readiness *in the moment*.

**F119 — The source's “first/worst/prototypical memory” targeting is a clinician strategy, not self-help instruction.** The book reports that a handful of clinical targets often suffice in their case-based protocol; it does not prove an unsupervised person ought to search for the earliest or worst childhood scene. The guide's warning against trying to manufacture or verify childhood memory from suggestive practice remains warranted.

**F120 — Termination in the illustrative patient case respected expressed sufficiency.** The case describes treatment ending after fewer than planned sessions when the patient said her goals had been met, partly supporting autonomy as a treatment outcome rather than a reason to keep processing indefinitely. The guide similarly resists self-perpetuating processing and encourages returning to life. Single-case observations do not establish an optimal course length for other people.

## Overall synthesis from five newly read chapters

- **Core equivalence expanded:** somatic attunement, protecting legitimate refusals, adjusting nurturing tone to actual reception, granting real autonomy, internalizing therapist/borrowed care, care received from the child’s perspective, appropriate external action, joining play, real-world goals, and voluntary endings are all explicitly present in at least some ST practitioners' descriptions.
- **Potential reader benefits with no new mandatory structure:** a brief “probes as inquiry, not affirmations” example for situations where care feels fake; one short scenario about a firm parent versus guilt-inducing parent requiring different protective responses; and the simple event/feeling/meaning distinction as an aid against confusing feelings with facts. **All require current-guide full-text comparison before an editorial proposal.**
- **Nuance to preserve rather than erase:** Chapters 4 and 6 explicitly support the client's right to decline imagery; some other instructions suggest working around refusal as an avoidant coping mode. The anthology is multi-author; distinguish a specific recommendation from a global ST principle.
- **Evidence limits:** the authors’ model, clinical vignettes, and explanatory neuroscience are not equivalent to independent mechanistic or outcome validation. No proof of memory recovery, objective “body wisdom”, universal optimal emotion intensity, 24/7 therapist availability, or efficacy of the owner’s full mixed self-directed method follows.
