## New findings F203–F224, grounded in van Genderen Chapter 6/conclusion/appendices

**F203 — The therapist deliberately *hands back* the parental roles.** Chapter 6 pp.147–153 and pp.211–213 describes the therapist transitioning from intervention to coaching while the person's Healthy Adult increasingly handles emotion, thought, practical decisions and boundaries. It is explicitly **borrowed/learned adulthood**, not an exclusively permanent relationship with a therapist. This is direct preexisting overlap with the owner's Nurturer/Protector/Guide and Adult Apprentice; no novelty claim.

**F204 — The four-step childhood scene shows both adult intervention and the child *receiving* care.** In the Martin vignette, the adult enters a childhood music-lesson disagreement, protects autonomy and comforts Little Martin, then the person returns to the child's vantage point to feel that intervention. The scene is imagined and does not establish what a historical parent would have thought or done. Strong overlap with borrowed care and trust through reception. Source pp.151–155.

**F205 — Modern-life boundaries are not an afterthought.** The workbook discusses changes in partners, work, job options, care obligations, social circle, competence and enjoyment (pp.148–151, 206–210). Its behavioral outcomes include saying no to requests, dividing chores fairly, refusing excessive overtime and leaving people who react with hostility. A reader must not be told Schema Therapy confines reparenting to inner comfort. The user's practical Protector is fully compatible.

**F206 — Future rehearsal is explicitly reality-limited.** The author asks the person to imagine a difficult conversation while embodying the Healthy Adult, then separately examine the real response (pp.157–166). In Nora's housework disagreement, the imagined partner remains uncooperative, and the therapist says a more skillful approach cannot guarantee a good outcome. This is excellent convergence with the user's distinction between an adult action and control of another person. The *format* of a brief specific if–then rehearsal may be useful for the guide; the concept is already present.

**F207 — Role reversing can reveal either person's role, but it remains a hypothesis.** The three-part recent-event role-play (pp.161–166) first tests the person's own response, then imagines the other's perspective and rehearses a clearer response. The authors concede the other person's behavior may independently be problematic or may require a mediator, rather than automatically psychologizing the client. But the other person's thoughts imagined in a role-play are **not facts about that person**. This caveat matters for blame, consent and abuser narratives.

**F208 — Coping parts are understood by their prior protective function before being asked to step aside.** In chair exercises (pp.166–171), the author differentiates abusive/one-sided self-criticism, which is challenged firmly, from avoidant/inversion coping, which is first thanked or understood as helping in a difficult earlier period. The goal is more context-appropriate Healthy Adult choice. This matches the owner's protector-sensitive sequence; the author's language of silencing/removing modes is sometimes more adversarial than the guide's.

**F209 — Thought journals separate justifiable distress from disproportionate predictions.** The thought exercise (pp.171–177) asks for the actual event, feeling, thought, behavior, role played by modes, **what was justified**, what may be exaggerated, and what the evidence shows. The example of an absent partner distinguishes the real absence from unsupported infidelity stories while planning practical check-ins. This is already in the guide's “Catch the Hook” and “Also Look Outward”; an optional single-card example might improve ease of use.

**F210 — The three explicit Healthy Adult operations are an extremely close match.** pp.179–182 guide one adult to **acknowledge emotion**, **evaluate faulty inferences**, and **choose an achievable, other-respecting action**; chairs can aid this but are not mandatory. These are the owner's **Nurturer, discernment-oriented Guide and action-oriented Protector** as functions of one adult. The distinction is packaging/sequence, not the fundamental model.

**F211 — Caring for the child is not restricted to talking about the past.** Printed pp.183–185 describes accepting present feelings, identifying the actual need, and **doing what is necessary now**, including changing an unsuitable job or relationship over time, seeking helpful people, noticing enjoyable activities, and using a simple symbolic reminder. It is a particularly strong convergence with the user's insistence that self-protective action is reparenting.

**F212 — Helping someone express anger differs from tolerating aggression.** The book distinguishes anger at real wrongs from coercive or impulsive entitlement (pp.185–198). It asks for early recognition, specific complaint, real boundary-setting and context-dependent intensity, alongside other people's rights. It sometimes recommends cathartic chair expression, but that should not be promoted as proven clinical treatment or a mandatory exercise. The user guide already distinguishes valid anger, action choice and actual non-harm.

**F213 — Three important safety qualifications in the anger passages.** First, the therapist recommends cooling down before intense re-enactment (pp.186–187). Second, the source sometimes attributes general physical symptoms or high blood pressure to suppressed anger (pp.193–194) **without testing causation in this book**: do not convert that to a diagnosis or suggest substituting expression for medical investigation. Third, someone's actual external dangerous acts remain subject to ordinary safety and legal consequences, not dissolved by an inner-mode story.

**F214 — The “impulsive/spoilt child” examples distinguish responsibility from humiliation.** The chapter uses the fictional partner's recurrent broken chores agreements to distinguish short-term gratification from longer-term damage; the proposed intervention is concrete, small, realistic goals, feedback, time limits, and consequences (pp.195–199). But a trained therapist cannot infer a client's childhood style solely from a preference, incomplete chore or executive-function impairment. Context, resources, goals and actual choices are critical.

**F215 — Reader joy is an active adult obligation.** pp.199–201 encourages small sensory and social pleasures, curiosity, friendship, music, games and relaxation even for people who have never learned their own preferences. The owner guide's “Let the Child Be Bad at Things” and child–adult reciprocity already contain this; neither joy nor playful self-experiment is a unique addition.

**F216 — A concrete behavioral experiment is one of the best *optional formats* to consider.** pp.204–205 directs the person to predict what will happen if they practice a valued new behavior, attempt a small bounded step, then compare the forecast with the observed result. This may help readers evaluate fears about saying no, asking for support or being imperfect. However a failed experiment may reflect a genuinely unsafe environment, inaccessible resources, power differences or bad prediction; **not every failed test proves a pathological mode.** The owner guide already suggests rehearsal, small acts and review; one compact worked example may add reader usability rather than theory.

**F217 — Source explicitly acknowledges social resistance to greater autonomy.** pp.206–210 describes friends offended by boundaries, colleagues forced to redistribute workloads, and a partner unwilling to honor agreements. Supportive practice does not require maintaining an exploitative arrangement for harmony. Nor is a loss of relationship automatically evidence of therapeutic failure. The user guide is aligned.

**F218 — A real domestic-harm concern appears later in the workbook.** The fictional Nora case (printed p.209) includes a black eye after severe partner conflict and a clinician urging her to change her situation; she then temporarily moves away. This demonstrates that **real external safety eventually matters in the protocol**, and that blaming her own “Witch” voice for the relationship cannot replace actual safety planning. This should not become a general plan to wait several psychotherapy phases before responding to violence, or a presumption that abused partners can safely negotiate changes. Compare owner's “Also Look Outward” and protective-intent gate.

**F219 — Some claims about adherence need contextual limits.** pp.201–208 describes the therapist sometimes firmly confronting a client who does not complete agreed homework. It can be helpful to examine legitimate avoidance, but exhaustion, disabilities, cost, transport, provider mistakes and coercive consequences may also block an action. The owner's guide already requires investigating *what happened* rather than classifying all noncompletion as unwillingness.

**F220 — The concluding chapter distinguishes improvement from a permanently pleasant life.** pp.232–234 says major adult decisions may produce temporary grief, loss or stress even when they improve fit with a person's needs, and modes may return during severe stress. This aligns with the guide's outcome-evaluation and transitional “bardo” material. The author's broad promised benefit should not be turned into an effect-size claim.

**F221 — The eight worksheets are operational aids, not diagnostic instruments.** Source pp.235–249 was read and rendered. They include: compact/expanded child–critic–coping/adult diagrams; a circle separating event, bodily signal, inferred thoughts/feelings, real need, action and outcome; a thought diary that asks which response was justified versus too strong; a source-specific imagery worksheet; three-stage role-play of another person's perspective; homework scheduling with potential obstacles; positive experiences log; and a before/after behavior review. All can help users, but **the user guide need not contain eight compulsory forms**. No worksheet proves that an imaginary parent explanation is historically correct.

**F222 — The most readily transferable *single* form is short: reality → feeling → need → next action → actual result.** This combines the self-inquiry circle with the homework/behavior-change form while preserving the owner's light-touch approach. It could be demonstrated with one original vignette (a real present conflict and one personal protective step) rather than forcing users into chair role-play or detailed inventories. This is a *proposal*, not an edited guide section.

**F223 — Therapist support is treated as a gradient, not a weakness.** Chapter 8 says a friend, primary-care mental health worker or schema therapist may be the right form of support depending on functioning and complexity; Chapter 6 and Step 5 have external help become more reciprocal while preserving useful care. The guide's borrowed-adult network and practical support already implement this principle. The self-help workbook does not authorize unsupervised reconstruction of severe abuse memories or clinical management of a violent relationship.

**F224 — Source precision matters for clinician and reader selection.** Some chapters describe psychological causes confidently from anecdotes, but other sections explicitly warn about rigid labels, individual variation, actual difficult environments, and professional assistance. Characterizing the full book as a single deterministic, coercive or nonconsensual method would disregard those qualifications. The same applies to the owner's guide: assess complete arguments, not isolated sentences.

## Cumulative three-book decision after main-text coverage

**Confirmed common ground** across Capacchione 1991, Young/clinical Schema Therapy 2003–2020, and van Genderen 2026: a vulnerable and happy child, critical learned attitudes, coping responses that may have protected in the past, a caring/protective/wise Healthy Adult, borrowed caregiver modeling, experiencing care *as* the child, play/creativity, current action and life-change, meaningful boundaries, relationship help, and long-run maintenance. Do not portray these as unique to the owner just because his terms are Nurturer/Protector/Guide.

**Differentiators supported as emphasis/integration, not historically guaranteed novelty:** explicit permission to reject the inner-child *frame itself*; medical/biographical/source uncertainty and anti-false-memory guardrails; the practical Protector *inside* reparenting even before formal mode work; a provisional, minimal internal taxonomy; a skeptical/fallible stance toward spiritual/intuition claims; and the user's three-role framing within a broader self-authored spiritual, community and adult-agency project.

**Top candidate usability elements requiring actual-current-guide review before drafting:**
1. An original concrete adult-to-child **apology** example, not a mandatory confession ritual (Capacchione).
2. A **single fear expressed through three coping strategies** (surrender/avoidance/inversion) to prevent simplistic mode identification (van Genderen).
3. A short **future boundary / if–then / prediction-versus-outcome rehearsal**, retaining realistic circumstances and help-seeking (van Genderen; anthology Chs.8,11).
4. A one-paragraph optional **facts, feelings, needs, action, outcome** exercise, rather than another checklist-heavy model (van Genderen).
5. Criteria for supportive professionals who respect refusal, own mistakes, avoid coerced disclosure and prepare for reasonable endings (anthology and Capacchione).

No addition should be proposed merely because it exists in a book. The dated October 4 guide already covers much of the mechanism; obtain current authoritative raw/humanized source if editing is later authorized.
