## Chapter 8 — current-life imagery (F121–F125)

**F121 — Current-life imagery does not require locating an early childhood memory.** The authors offer recently experienced scenes, live feelings and future situations as three entry points. A therapist can help the patient differentiate anger, fear and adult perspective while keeping the external event unchanged. Case: a supervisor fails to acknowledge an employee; fear of losing a job rises quickly, while anger and shame are also present. The imagined scene helps the client understand and respond, without declaring that any particular historic origin has been found. **Guide disposition: strong convergence** with noticing present triggers before interpretation and checking external reality; no obligation to “float back” to childhood.

**F122 — Present-focused imagery can borrow care when the patient cannot supply it.** In the chapter's Gabriella case, a supportive adult stands behind a frightened/hunched child in imagery; therapist support is added when the patient needs it, and the patient then adopts the child's viewpoint to see how support actually feels. This is extraordinarily close to the guide’s borrowed-adult / Nurturer and care-received model. The authors do not equate a physical sensation with proof of a childhood event. **Already present in concept.**

**F123 — Future imagery is more than optimistic visualization; it includes obstacles and an if–then response.** The chapter cites *mental contrasting* (juxtapose a desired outcome with realistic barriers) and *implementation intentions* (name a specific behavior to perform if a barrier occurs). Case: someone imagines enrolling in art classes, realizes a discouraging phone response and self-deflating thoughts could derail the plan, and chooses a concrete reminder/action for that scenario. This is supported by an *existing distinct behavioral-planning literature* cited by the chapter; the efficacy of the whole schema-imagery intervention for all clients was **not established by the presented cases**. **Potential small, optional guide worked example:** “I want to do X; likely obstacle Y; if Y, I will do Z”; then test actual outcome. Existing guide already advises small acts, rehearsal and review. Do not add a mandatory program.

**F124 — Imagined retaliation is not safe conduct by default.** The chapter warns that anger toward real-life contemporaries rehearsed in imagery can spill into action; it recommends separating fantasy from behavior, a moderated adult response, and debriefing, particularly with impulsivity. This aligns with the guide's separation of internal reaction, chosen action, consent and immediate safety. **No new conceptual gap.**

**F125 — Treatment preference needs more than “you're avoidant.”** The authors sometimes introduce recent-life imagery as a step toward later childhood work, but explicitly allow current-focused work for its own regulatory/goal benefit. Wanting to avoid painful history need not be pathologized or used to pressure a person toward one definitive technique. **Preserve the guide's stronger right to reject the entire framework.**

## Chapter 9 — child/critic dialogues (F126–F130)

**F126 — “Dysfunctional Critic” is deliberately broader than “bad internalized parent.”** Farrell and Shaw choose the term because internal criticisms may also originate with teachers, peers and coaches, and labeling everything “Parent” may provoke unfounded family-loyalty disputes. The guide already acknowledges learned critical scripts and protects against automatic blame/identity assignments. **Potential terminology note, not new theoretical entity.**

**F127 — Vicarious learning is an explicit graduated borrowed-adult route.** When a patient's Healthy Adult is weak or the punitive voice terrifying, the person can start by observing a therapist or peers protect a hypothetical/represented child, gradually stand beside a supporter, then speak for the adult themselves. A patient need not immediately take every role. **Strong convergence** with the guide's borrowed Nurturer/Protector/Adult Apprentice and practice in relationship. A simple original example of “watch → join → try” could be useful, but the principle is not novel. Group demonstrations include safety positioning and choice of participation.

**F128 — Some variants deliberately dramatize the Critic as a monster/effigy to expel.** This can give clients a powerful experience of rebuffing abuse, according to clinical examples, but it differs in tone from the owner's habit of treating inner responses provisionally and understanding protective strategies without creating an adversarial internal cast. The text also says the internalized critic is often only negative learned messaging, not the entire original parent. **Method divergence**, not proof one procedure is always better. Do not prescribe destruction, shouting or confrontation as a safe default, especially when internal fragmentation/dissociation is salient.

**F129 — The “birth of the modes” group drama is a model illustration, not a reliable reconstruction of anyone's past.** The authors make unmet needs, angry child, critic and coping strategies all speak at once to demonstrate why the child feels unheard. It is a vivid teaching heuristic; the prescribed sequence is **a theory** not proof every person developed internal states in that order. Related historical/collective mode dialogues and verbal role-play can teach observation but may be too intense or socially pressured for some participants. **Avoid presenting it as a universal psychological timeline.**

**F130 — Two anecdotal exercises illustrate borrowed compassion, but not controlled efficacy.** A therapist plays an unprotected child so a highly punitive client can feel ordinary compassion toward *that* child and transfer it inward; another exercise compares doing a tricky task with a critic's discouragement versus encouraging statements. The latter is confounded by repeated attempts and expectancy; both are case demonstrations rather than controlled effect estimates. **Potential optional observational exercise only after safety/consent review.** The authors advise final grounding and safety planning, and recommend therapists avoid playing the patient's severely punitive parent role.

## Chapter 10 — play and joy (F131–F135)

**F131 — Play and shared delight are explicitly limited-reparenting treatment functions, not entertainment after “real work.”** Ida Shaw argues that play supports safety, spontaneity, social skills and positive attachment, and asks the therapist to bring an authentic happy/playful adult rather than a technician administering games. This strongly overlaps the guide's Nurturer who *enjoys* the child, the reciprocity of child–adult vitality, and “pl/ork.” **Do not claim originality of joy-as-therapy.**

**F132 — Therapeutic play can be a gentle “landing” into relationship.** In the Lego vignette, a withdrawn patient starts sessions by building and eventually moves from relaxed play into verbal work. The object is not interpreted as a hidden trauma diagnostic; it gives a predictable shared activity and a way to feel comfortable. **Possible practical example** for people who dislike starting with dialogue; this is not a newly missing adult function. It could also fit a safe peer format, without converting the owner's Hearthwork into therapist-like interpretation.

**F133 — Group play is organized collaboration with real competence.** A shared dollhouse/safe-house project involves budgets, tools, delegating, design disagreements and celebrating accomplishment; imagined toy-store visits, games and personal keepsake boxes are options, not universal necessities. This reinforces that play can develop functional adult skills while allowing childlike pleasure. The guide already promotes expressive experiments and community. **Convergence.**

**F134 — There is an autonomy tension when play is used to “bypass” defenses.** The chapter acknowledges that play may be threatening, embarrassing or unwanted and recommends patience and observing from a distance; elsewhere it portrays play as a route *around* a protector. Preserve the critical distinction between genuine willingness and reluctant participation, especially when the person explicitly does not want the inner-child frame. **Guide's consent boundary remains stronger and should not be displaced.**

**F135 — The chapter's use of a dissociative-identity case is not an invitation to equate alters with schema modes.** The author interpreted a patient's young alter as the Vulnerable Child in one case. This is a case-specific therapeutic interpretation, not a universal taxonomy for dissociative conditions. Likewise play-based vigorous anger expression is not proven to resolve aggression across populations. **Keep specialized clinical contexts outside a mandatory self-help exercise.**

## Chapter 11 — coping modes and chair work (F136–F141)

**F136 — Coping styles are portrayed as useful *capacities taken too far*.** The authors name sensitivity to needs, threat detection, perseverance, organization, fighting strength, ambition and retreating to regroup; the aim is flexible moderation, not elimination of every protective response. This is a particularly strong convergence with the guide's understanding of protective parts and realistic adult protection. **No new theoretical role.**

**F137 — Therapist rupture repair is genuinely reciprocal.** A patient reacts to a formal warning letter as threatening loss of care; the therapist notices both her own shame/withdrawal and the patient's protective response, hears the complaint, acknowledges that the letter landed badly, apologizes, and they plan how to communicate better. The example contains *both* legitimate hurt and potentially habitual withdrawal, neither cancels the other. **This supports the guide's “Also Look Outward” and correctable-helper principle**; not an absence in Schema Therapy.

**F138 — Avoidant coping can create an “I must but I can't” bind.** Pressuring behavior change while a harsh inner critic undermines agency may strengthen avoidance. The authors instead have a frightful child articulate needs, the healthy adult rehearse specific next steps, and the protector keep a limited standby role. This is similar to the guide's sequence: hear the guard, build an adult, take a realistic action. **Potential worked example of when not to push past fear**, not a new framework.

**F139 — The client's real refusal of coercive intimacy can be the Healthy Adult's starting point.** In one case, someone feels obliged to date a friend because the friend once helped secure employment. The chair dialogue brings out a younger part's clear opposition; the adult validates their autonomy and helps form a plan. The vignette does not show every eventual result. **Strong convergence:** real consent and real-world boundaries are reparenting, not separate from it.

**F140 — Overcompensation is neither automatically evil nor always safe.** The authors suggest integrating useful strength from an aggressive or controlling pattern while limiting intimidation and illegal conduct. A worked dialogue features compulsive surveillance/checking of a supervisor's email; the person's adult response validates anxiety *without* endorsing improper snooping, plans to tolerate uncertainty and protect livelihood. **Convergent with owner guide's moral accountability and not confusing feelings with permissible acts.**

**F141 — Two diagrams serve as descriptive tools, not measured brain structures.** Fig.11.1 maps critic, Healthy Adult, numbness/toughness and vulnerable child using a hand-drawn scene; Fig.11.2 follows “harsh critic → scared child → escape response → adult reassessment” for a panic attack on a bus. The clinical examples involve additional hypotheses. This supports a single clear *illustrative* pathway if needed; it does not establish the true cause of every symptom. **Do not proliferate mandatory modes.**

## Chapter 12 — forensic/other clinical settings (F142–F147)

**F142 — Therapist boundaries also matter, even when the patient's feelings are valid.** The authors distinguish subjective anger/protest from intimidation, physical aggression and deliberate coercive actions. When someone throws objects or threatens a therapist, the proposed response is a firm, nonpunitive stop and respectful limits, followed by understanding and repair where workable. Tables 12.1 and 12.2 distinguish child, critic and coping modes and give tailored clinical interventions. **Owner guide convergence:** Nurturer receives experience, Protector stops harmful acts, Guide chooses appropriate next actions.

**F143 — Important model overreach: the requested therapist switch is not automatically a “Self-Aggrandiser.”** In a vignette, a patient prefers a woman therapist and the authors declare that a power-positioning coping mode is probably active. The source does not establish what the patient actually needs, prior treatment history or whether the requested transfer is reasonable. Compare Ch.4, which respects legitimate refusal and preferences; an interpretation that the request is defensive should be evaluated rather than used to overrule the choice. **Preserve an actual right to change therapists and to reject an exercise**. This is a tension *within the anthology*, not evidence all schema therapy ignores autonomy.

**F144 — Mode labels are hypotheses, not reliable classifiers from expressions alone.** The chapter proposes differentiating visible anger, covert hostility, intimidation and cold instrumental manipulation; this can draw attention to behavior and its consequences but does not give a validated rule to infer hidden intent from a facial expression or preference. Case details must be grounded in actual acts, responses to limits and other corroboration. **Guide remains evidence-first, not label-first.**

**F145 — The therapist can enter a destructive cycle too.** The authors describe three therapist–patient mode patterns: both withdraw intellectually/emotionally (congruence), one dominates while the other submits (complementarity), and both compete for control (mode battle). This expands their account beyond “client resistance” and highlights professional self-monitoring. The proposed adult response is to notice one's own activation, pause, ground, and choose an intervention while taking responsibility for actual mistakes. **Guide convergence:** no infallible therapist-guru; genuine misattunement is not proof of a pathological client part.

**F146 — Cases about harmful intent are specialized.** The authors link certain “forensic” modes to violent or exploitative acts, but the reader should not be instructed to diagnose a Predator mode from an ordinary ambiguous disagreement. The guide's separate safety gate for *currently endorsed intent to exploit vulnerability* should not be replaced with speculative inner identities. **Preserve behavior/intent and protection as the operative safety test.**

**F147 — The book's trial claims are temporally bounded.** It references a forensic RCT “submitted” at the time of writing and reports better-than-usual results from it without details in this chapter. That is an author's description of an unpublished/submitted study at the time; **not an independently verified trial finding in this pass**. Clinical case illustrations in the chapter are not validation of precise mode attribution or the owner's combined method.

## What the owner guide might use (ranking is provisional)

1. **One explicit “obstacle → response” future-action rehearsal** (Ch.8). The dated guide already advises rehearsal and small protective acts; the novel *presentation detail* is a concrete implementation-intention example. It could help readers progress from feeling cared for to acting, without demanding perfect readiness.
2. **An optional shared/playful “landing” activity before difficult conversation** (Ch.10), to illustrate that nourishment and mutual delight can be part of connection, not merely a recreational add-on. Underlying theory already present, so do not suggest a new obligatory section.
3. **A small “notice what was protective and preserve the skill” example** (Ch.11) distinguishing adaptive vigilance/strength from repetitive surveillance, coercion or avoidance. The dated guide already says to hear protectors; no automatic theory gap.
4. **A professional-help selection boundary** (Ch.12): patient preferences, refusals and realistic safety may be valid; a clinician ought to welcome correction rather than automatically assigning a patient a defensive mode. The owner guide says this already, but an example may make it practical.

**Do not transfer** unconsented confrontation; staged “demon” expulsion; forced play; therapeutic mislabelling of an authentic sexual/relational preference; illegal monitoring; physical attacks; suggestive recovered-memory work; or therapist-specific trauma procedures into generic independent self-reparenting. These exclusions come from a comparison of the chapter cases and the owner's stated guide boundaries, not a claim that all these therapeutic activities are inherently harmful.
